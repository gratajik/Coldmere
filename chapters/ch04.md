# Chapter 4: The Counting

The dent had unsettled me more than I let it. I went back down to the front room and the lamps and lay in my coat and made myself list the day's work, which is what I do when a thing won't sit flat. Six rooms downstairs, one a cupboard. Four up, plus the room the plan forgot. Tomorrow I would tag.

I was nearly under when the counting started.

At first it was nothing. A knock in the wall, the kind a cold house gives all night as the timber moves and the pipes tick down. I'd heard it in a hundred houses. I lay still and let it be that.

Then it came again, and it wasn't a knock.

It was a voice. Low, in the wall behind my head, pitched so even I'd have missed it under the knock if the knock hadn't quieted to let it through. A man's voice, or near it, flat and unhurried, and it was saying a number.

"Thirty-seven."

I sat up.

The room held its amber and its corners. Nothing moved. I kept my hand on the bedroll and waited, and told myself it was the pipes, water in a run of copper making a sound the ear shapes into a word, the way you hear a name in the rush of a tap.

"Thirty-eight."

A pause. A long one, the length of a held breath. Then, after a knock in the pipe, low and even:

"Thirty-nine."

I got up. I took the lamp from the floor and held it to the wall behind the bedroll, the way you hold a torch to a plan when the total is wrong. Plaster. A picture-hook, empty. Below it the skirting, and behind the skirting the box the phone cord ran to, and behind that the wall and whatever ran inside it. I put my ear to the cold plaster and the lamp threw my shadow up the wall, and I listened.

The house ticked. The pipe knocked. And under it, patient, the voice went on.

"Forty."

I knew that cadence. I've stood at the back of a hundred auction rooms with my conditions and my lot list while a man at the front worked a sale, and that was the sound of it. Not a man reading. A man calling. The flat even pace of an inventory gone through, lot by lot, the pause where the eye lifts to the next item before the mouth gives it a number.

"Forty-one."

I stood back from the wall.

There's a dull explanation for everything, and I've built a life on reaching for it. Water in the pipes. The wind in the eaves shaping itself to sound. A radio left on in a far room, a frequency the old wiring carried.

I went to the door and out to the hall and stood under the stopped clock and listened to the whole dark house at once, to find the radio, the far room, the open window. There was nothing. The house was silent, except where it counted.

"Forty-two."

It was coming from the wall I'd left, and from above it, faint, as though the count ran up through the house from the front room to the landing and the room the plan forgot. I didn't go up to find it.

I went back in and took out my notebook and sat in the lamplight with the pen and made myself write, because writing a thing down is the one act that has never failed me.

Condition: a counting in the walls. Upstairs and behind the front-room skirting. A man's voice, even, the cadence of a sale called lot by lot. Begun, I noted, somewhere near thirty-seven. Rising.

I sat with the pen and the number thirty-seven and felt the cold of the floor come up into me, and I didn't write down the thing I'd half-thought, which was that a sale called lot by lot is a count that climbs to a total, and that the man at the front of the room knows the total before he starts.

I wrote the rational lines instead. Old plumbing. The wind. A frequency carried on the wiring. I underlined none of them, because I did not believe them, and I do not underline what I do not believe.

The count went on behind the wall while I wrote, and I didn't let myself hear which number it had reached.
