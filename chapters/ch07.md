# Chapter 7: The Sealed Lot

I went back to the study after the back rooms, with the light failing and the marbles cold in my pocket, because the ledgers were on the list and the list was all I had. The case stood where it had stood, glass-fronted, the blank spines in their even rows. I'd left them for last. I meant to keep that order. Order was the railing.

But the crates were in the dining room, and I'd packed eleven of them that morning, and something in me wanted to count them again before the dark came down. So I went to the dining room first. I want to set down why, plainly, because the reason matters to what came after. I wanted to stand in front of a finished thing.

Eleven crates against the wall. I counted them by lamplight. Eleven. Each one sealed, each one with its manifest on the lid in my own hand, each seam cut square. A finished thing.

I stood and faced them the way you face a wall you've built, and for a breath I had the morning's ease back, the clean rhythm of it, the proof that I could take a room apart and make it hold.

Then I saw the seal.

The fourth crate from the left. The tape across its seam was cut, the square end I cut every time lifted and curled back from the cardboard, the way tape curls when a thumb has worked under it and peeled. I knew that crate.

Dining, plate, the lid said. The canteen of silver in its baize rolls. I had taped it and cut it and pressed it flat with my thumb that morning, and I had set it against the wall fourth from the left, and the seal had been whole.

It wasn't whole now.

I stood very still. A seal can lift. Tape loses its grip in the cold, the adhesive going brittle, the cut end peeling of its own accord off old cardboard. I have seen it a hundred times in cold houses. That is what I made myself believe, standing there, and I went to the crate and crouched and brought the lamp down close against the seal.

It had not peeled. The cut end was lifted clean, turned back in a neat curl, and under it the cardboard flaps stood a half-inch apart, sprung open the way a packed crate springs when the weight inside has shifted and pushed the lid. Not let go. Opened.

And then, I saw, not closed again, because the flaps didn't sit down flush the way I'd left them. They stood proud. Somebody had had the lid up and set it back and not pressed it.

I am not a fanciful woman. I crouched in the cold beside the open seam and made myself reason it. The silver had shifted. The baize rolls had settled in the night and pushed the flaps and sprung the seal, and the seal had lifted, and the flaps had risen, and that was the whole of it, a packed crate in a cold room doing what packed crates do.

I lifted the flaps the rest of the way to check the silver. I count what I pack. I would count it again and find the canteen whole and the count right and put the lid back down and press it, and the railing would hold.

The baize rolls were as I'd packed them. The spoons in their roll, the forks, the canteen sound, nothing shifted, nothing fallen, the whole of it lying as I'd laid it that morning, snug, undisturbed. Nothing had moved in the crate.

The silver hadn't pushed the lid. The silver hadn't sprung the seal. The crate was packed exactly as I'd packed it, and the seal was cut, and the lid was up.

And on top of the silver, on the baize, where I had packed no such thing, there was a ball.

A red rubber ball. Small, the size of a child's fist, the rubber gone soft and dull with age, a child's ball, the kind a child bounces off a wall and catches. It sat on the baize rolls in the open crate, on top of the silver I had counted into it that morning, under a seal I had cut with my own knife, in a crate I had set against the wall and not touched since.

I didn't pick it up. I knelt with the lamp at my knee and studied it, and I felt the count start in me, my own count, the steadying one, and it found nothing to count, because there was one ball and one is not a number you count to. One.

I'll set down what I did, because it is the only honest thing I have. I reached out and put one finger against the ball.

It gave, the soft give of old rubber, and it was warm. Not the warmth of a thing in a warm room. The warmth of a thing held. The warmth of a ball that has been in a hand.

I took my finger off it.

I have packed three hundred houses. I have sealed ten thousand crates and cut every seam square and pressed every lid flat with my own thumb, and in thirty years I have never once opened a crate I sealed and found in it a thing I did not pack. The seal is the whole of the trust. The seal is the work.

You pack the thing and you count it and you write it on the lid and you draw the tape and cut it square, and the crate is closed, and what is in it is what you put in it, and that is the floor I have stood on my whole life, that a closed thing holds what you put in it and nothing else.

The floor was gone. I knelt on it and it wasn't there.

I made myself take up the manifest, the lid in my own hand. Dining, plate & glass. The canteen, the count of pieces, the lot number.

There was no ball on the manifest. There was no ball in the crate when I sealed it. I would have written it. I write what I pack.

I wrote five wine glasses because there were five, and one missing, and I noted the one missing, because I note what isn't there. A red rubber ball on a canteen of silver is not a thing I would have packed and not written. It is not a thing I would have packed at all. There was no child's ball in the dining room. There was no child's ball in the house but the toys at the dead end of the upstairs passage, on a shelf, in the child's room I had catalogued that afternoon.

A spinning top. A wooden horse. A picture book. A tin of marbles, nineteen, one red, that I had put in my pocket.

No ball. There had been no ball on the shelf. I had inventoried that shelf by lamplight not three hours before and written down what was on it and there had been no ball.

I knelt in the cold and the lamp shook its small gold on the red rubber and I couldn't make it a lot.

That is the thing I have to set down, because it is the whole of the wrongness and I am a woman whose trade is to find the column for any object in any house. I could note it. Red rubber ball, child's, perished, found in a sealed crate. I could write the words. But the work is not the words.

The work is that the words make the thing make sense, that you write red rubber ball and tie the tag and the ball goes into the order of the house and stops being a thing that frightens you. And this ball would not go into the order. There was no order it fit. It was in a crate I had sealed, under a seal I had cut, and there was no way for it to be there, and writing it down did not make a way.

I wrote it down anyway.

I took the manifest off the lid and turned it over and wrote on the back, because the front was the truth I'd sealed and this was a different truth. Crate 4, dining plate. Seal cut. Lid up, flaps proud.

Silver whole, undisturbed, as packed. And on the silver: a red rubber ball, child's, perished. Warm. Not on manifest.

Not packed. I have not opened this crate since sealing it. My hand wasn't steady writing it and I let the hand show.

I did not close the crate. I want that on the record too. I knelt there a long while with the lid up and the ball on the silver, and I couldn't bring myself to put the lid down on it and press it flat, because pressing it flat was sealing it in, and I had sealed this crate once already with my own thumb and it hadn't held what I put in it, and I wouldn't do it again.

I wouldn't pretend the seal meant anything. So I left the crate open, fourth from the left, the lid up, the ball on the baize, and I stood, and my knees cracked with the cold, and I took the lamp and stepped back.

The marbles were in my pocket. Nineteen, one red. I'd taken the red one out of a tin upstairs not three hours ago, and now there was a red ball downstairs in a crate I'd sealed, and the two facts sat in me side by side and wouldn't be kept apart, and I felt the lid of the drawer I keep shut push up against my hand from the inside.

A boy who always took the red one.

I held the lamp and I didn't say the name. I wouldn't say it in that house. But I knew, kneeling there hadn't let me not know, that the seal had not lifted in the cold and the silver hadn't shifted in the night. The crate had been opened by a hand and a thing put in it, gently, on the silver, where I would find it, a red thing, a warm thing, a thing for a child's hand, and the lid set back not quite pressed so that I would see it had been opened and know it had been opened and understand that the sealing of a thing in this house meant nothing at all.

I went and slept in the kitchen with the lamp up and the crate open behind me in the dark, and I didn't write down the thing I understood, which was that I had stopped being the one who sealed the crates.
