# Chapter 7: The Sealed Lot

I returned to the front reception to fetch the chest I'd sealed near noon, because the manifest had it as lot five and I'd written lot five, and a chest can't be two numbers.

That was the whole of it. A clerical slip. I'd numbered the study chest five and then the reception chest five too, and a manifest with two fives in it is a manifest I can't stand behind. I crouched by the chest just inside the doorway and bent the buff card up to read what I'd written.

The study chest. I'd brought it through and set it here and forgotten I had.

I checked the seal anyway. It is the first thing I check. My own paper tape, cut from my own roll, laid over the lid and signed across the join with the date.

The signature was mine, the ink dark where I'd pressed it. The join was whole. No one had been at it. I knew that before I looked, because I am the only one in the house, but I looked.

Then I cut it.

I cut my own seals with a short blade I keep stropped, drawn along the join so the tape parts and the signature with it, and the signature once parted is a thing spent. I drew it now. The tape opened. The date tore through the middle of my own name.

I lifted the lid.

Glassware, wrapped in the newspaper I'd packed it in. The clock without works. The box of letters. All as I'd logged them, the manifest correct, the chest accounted for.

And on top, where nothing had been when I'd signed the lid, a ball.

A red rubber ball. Child's size, the rubber gone matte and soft with age, a seam round its middle worn nearly smooth. It sat on the newspaper as if set down a moment before.

I didn't touch it.

I sat back on my heels with the lid in my hand and the cut seal hanging and looked at it. Red. The rubber kind, the kind that fits a child's palm and bounces dead on stone. I'd had one. Every child had.

I went over it the only way I know.

The seal was mine. The tape was mine, cut from my roll. The signature was mine and the date was the day and the join had been whole; I'd checked it before I cut it and it had not been opened.

I packed this chest myself. I wrapped each piece and laid it and there was no ball in it. I would have logged a ball. I log everything. A ball goes on the manifest as a ball.

I made myself say it flat. The chest was sealed by my own hand under my own signed and dated join. The join was unbroken. And a thing was inside it that I had not put there.

I have an explanation for everything. I reached for one and there was none. A misremembered packing wouldn't account for it. A second chest wouldn't, nor a slipped seal, because I'd checked the seal and the seal was whole. The fact would not square, and there was no line I could rule under it to make it square.

I took out my notebook. My hand was steady. I am proud of that. I wrote the date and the lot and what I found, and the words came out short, the way they do when a thing won't sit.

Condition: sealed by me, join unbroken, signed and dated in my hand. Found within, on examination: one red rubber ball, child's. Not packed. Not logged.

I read it back. It was true and it was impossible, and I have spent my life on the rule that those two cannot both stand. I wrote it down anyway.

I did not write the rest. The marbles down the passage, in their tin, in the room kept for a boy of seven. A red ball is a thing a boy keeps with his marbles. A red ball is a thing that goes in the tin, or near it, in the room I'd shut the door on an hour before.

I closed the lid on it without touching the ball. I couldn't bring myself to lay my hand on a thing that had no provenance, that had come from nowhere into a place I'd sealed against nowhere.

I stayed there a while with the chest shut and the cut seal hanging and the manifest open on my knee, and I could not bring the column to a total, and outside the light was going off the pond.
