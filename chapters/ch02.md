# Chapter 2: Lamplight

The lamps came out of my bag before the dark fully owned the room. Two paraffin lamps, my own, because I have cleared enough houses to know the power can fail at the worst hour, and the bank's wiring was already telling me it would. I set one on the mantel and one on the floor by my bedroll. The flame steadied. The room came up amber and small, the corners further off than the lamplight reached.

I am methodical about a first night. Bedroll near the front room's hearth. Bag squared against the wall where I can find it blind. Boots by the door, laced loose.

I don't unpack what I won't need. A house I am clearing is not a place I live; it is a place I am inside of for the duration of the work.

The cold was the thing. Not sharp, the way outside had been sharp, but old. It came up through the floorboards and stood in the room with me. I kept my coat on. I have slept in colder.

I should have called in. That was the next item, and I am good about items. The bank wanted a start confirmed, and my own habit is to log a first-night check with the office, a date and a time and a line that says the work has begun. There was no signal in the house. My phone had shown two bars at the second cattle-grid and none past the third, and none here.

I'd marked that on the drive and thought nothing of it. The bank's letter had said landline, and the long-case clock wasn't the only thing in the hall.

The telephone was on a half-table by the foot of the stairs, an old cream handset, the kind with a dial. The cord ran to a box on the skirting. I lifted the receiver and put it to my ear out of plain procedure, the way you test a tap before you trust the water.

There was a tone.

I noted that with something close to satisfaction. A working line. I'd call the office, log the start, and that would be the day closed and accounted for. I put my finger to the dial.

Then I stopped, because the tone was wrong.

Not the sound of it. The sound was ordinary, the flat single note of a line waiting to be used. It was the timing. I'd lifted the receiver, and the tone hadn't been there, and then it had been there, a half-second after, as if it had been switched on for me. As if the line had been silent and had heard the receiver come up and decided, late, to answer.

I lowered it from my ear and looked at it. A dial tone does not begin. It is simply there the instant the circuit closes, the way a light is there the instant you throw the switch. This one had begun. I'd heard the silence first.

Condition: the line was live but slow. I told myself the exchange was old, the relays out here run down, eleven miles of single copper laid before the war and never replaced, a lag in the switching gear that any engineer would shrug at. That's the dullest explanation, and the dull explanation is nearly always the true one. I've built a whole working life on it.

I lifted the receiver again to prove it to myself. Silence. One beat of silence with the cold handset against my ear and the dark hall around me. Then, late, the tone.

I dialled the office. It rang, and the ringing too seemed to reach me a fraction after I expected it, though by then I couldn't have said whether that was the line or my own listening, gone careful. No one answered. After hours. I left no message; there was no machine to leave it on, only the ringing into an empty office and the slow line carrying it.

I put the receiver back on its cradle. The click of it was the only certain thing in the room.

I wrote it down anyway. Landline live, response delayed, office unmanned, start unlogged. I underlined the third word, the way I underline anything that won't yet sit flat.

Then I went back to the lamplight and lay down in my coat, and listened to a house that, when I held still, didn't seem to be holding quite as still as I was.
