# Chapter 9: Footprints

The case stayed closed. I went out of the study in the last of the daylight, meaning to finish the measuring while the light held, and I stopped on the bottom tread with my hand on the newel, because the stairs were wet.

Not all of them. Two.

I put the lamp up. The fourth tread from the bottom held a print, a small one, the shape of a child's bare foot pressed in water, dark on the dry old oak, toe-marks and a heel and the little gap of an instep. And above it, on the seventh, another, the right foot to the left below it, a stride apart, climbing.

I stood and I counted them, the way I count to hold the ground. One on the fourth. One on the seventh. Two prints. I looked up the flight for the rest and there weren't any. Two wet prints, low on the stairs, and the treads above them dry, and the treads below them dry, and the hall floor I'd crossed a minute before bone-dry under my own boots.

I crouched. I am thorough and the work was the railing and I would do the work on this as I do the work on everything. I brought the lamp down close to the lower print.

It was water. I touched the edge of it with one finger and the finger came away wet, plain water, cold, no smell to it, the print of a small wet foot on a dry stair. The sole-shape was clear, the five round toes, the ball, the heel, a child's foot, seven years to it, the same size as the bed at the dead end of the passage, the same size as the grey flannel shorts folded in the drawer.

I am not a fanciful woman. There is water in an old house. A pipe weeps in the wall, a slate's gone and the wet comes down the chimney, condensation gathers on cold oak in a cold house and stands in beads. That is the reasoning and I made myself walk through it crouched on the stairs with the lamp at my knee, and the reasoning broke on the shape, because condensation does not have toes.

I knelt there and I watched it. The print was drying. Not how a spill dries, not fading even all over at once, going pale in the middle while the edges hold last. This dried from the outside in.

The rim of it went first. The clear wet line of the heel-edge and the toe-edge thinned and lifted off the oak and went dry, and the dryness crept inward toward the center, the print shrinking on itself, smaller and smaller, the way the tide goes off a stone and leaves a last dark coin of wet in the middle, until there was a small dark patch the size of a thumbnail, and then there was nothing, and the tread was dry.

I watched a wet footprint dry from its edges to its middle on a stair in a cold house and I did not have a word for it, and finding the word is the only thing I have ever been able to do.

The upper print did the same. I'd turned the lamp on it and it was already going at the rim, the heel-edge lifting, the dry creeping in, and I knelt and watched the second print close on itself and vanish as the first had, outside to in, and then the stairs were dry, all of them, and I was a woman crouched alone on a dry flight with a lamp.

I should say what I did not do. I did not put the lamp down. I did not go and check the landline or the cattle-grid or the meter. I knelt on the fourth tread where a foot had been, and I felt the lid of the drawer I keep shut push up under my hand from the inside, hard, because a small wet bare foot climbing the stairs of a house with no child in it is a thing I have a name for, and the name was Danny, and I would not say it, not on the stairs, not for anything.

A boy who went into water and never came out of it.

I stood up. My knees were stiff and the cold had got into them and I held the newel and stood, and I looked up the dark of the flight to where it turned at the half-landing, and I made myself do the only thing left to do, which was follow them.

You follow the evidence. That is the work. A print is a thing that points and you go where it points and you note what stands at the trail's end. So I went up the eleven treads with the lamp, one foot then the other on the wet places that were dry now, the fourth and the seventh, and there were no more prints above the seventh, none on the half-landing, none on the four treads after, none on the wide cold landing at the top. The trail stopped at the seventh stair.

But I knew where it went. I knew it on the bottom tread and I knew it climbing and I would not let myself know it until I was standing on the landing with the doors in front of me, the two bedrooms and the bathroom and the box room, all of them shut, as I'd left them, all of them giving back the same flat weeks-old cold, and one stretch of inner wall that wasn't a door and was warm.

The prints had been climbing. Right foot, left foot, a stride apart, the gait of a child going up. Going up to where. There was one warm thing in this house and it stood behind that papered seam, and a small wet thing climbing the stairs in the dusk had been climbing toward it, toward the chair and the window and the dent that fills.

I went to the seam. I put my hand flat on the wallpaper an inch from the hidden edge of the door, as you put your hand to a chimney-breast to feel the fire on the other side, and the wall was warm, the same blood-warmth as the room behind it, the only warm wall in eleven cold miles.

I did not open it. I want that on the record. My hand was on the warm wall and the door would have swung in on its silent hinge under two fingers and I stood with my hand flat on the paper and I did not push it.

Because I knew what was in there. The chair. The footstool. The side table with the unlit lamp. The window with the curtains drawn back, looking down the dark slope to the water. Edith's chair, and the dent in the cushion that comes back when you press it flat, and nothing else, nothing I could tag, nothing I could weigh.

Whatever had climbed the stairs in water, drying as it went, had gone into the one warm room in the house, and I'd find the room exactly as I'd left it, warm and empty, the chair holding its weight that isn't there, and that was worse than finding a thing in it. A thing in it I could note. An empty warm room a wet child had climbed to and was not in was a thing with no column at all.

I took my hand off the wall.

I went back down the eleven treads with the lamp, slowly, and I looked at every tread as I went, the fourth and the seventh and all the rest, and they were dry, every one, dry oak in a dry stairwell, no sign that water had ever stood on them, no sign that I had knelt and watched two prints close from their edges to their middles and disappear. There was nothing to photograph. There was nothing to tag. There was only what I had seen, and what I had seen was already gone off the wood as if it had never been, the way the whole house gave me things and then took the proof of them back.

I sat at the kitchen table and I opened the notebook and I made my hand take up the pencil, because this one I'd log, this one I'd keep. I set it all down in the book. The time, dusk. The place, the stairs. Two prints, the fourth tread and the seventh, a child's bare foot, seven years, near enough, water, climbing. And then the thing I had watched, that I noted because it was the truest and the worst, that they dried from the outside in, rim first and the dry creeping to the middle, and were gone.

I sat with the pencil and I made myself write the rest, the part the work needed, where the evidence pointed.

The prints climbed. They stopped at the seventh stair. There were none above it.

But a thing that climbs is going somewhere, and there is one warm place in this house, and a small wet thing climbing in the dark was climbing to the warm room. I put my hand on the wall. The wall was warm. I did not open the door.

I read it back. It held together as a record, the time and the place and the shape and the strange drying, the trail and where it pointed, all of it ordered, all of it noted, a clean account of a thing I could not file. And beneath it, because I keep the record of what I cannot bear so as to stay the one holding the pencil, I set down the last line, plain, and did not underline it this time, because underlining it would not have made it any truer than it already was.

Something climbs the stairs to the warm room. It went up tonight in the wet. It was the size of a child.

I put the pencil down. The lamp burned. And I sat with the marbles cold in my pocket against my leg, nineteen of them, one red, and I did not say the name of the boy whose foot was that size, who went into the water once and was never found, and who had climbed the stairs tonight, dripping, toward the only warm room in his great-aunt's freezing house.
