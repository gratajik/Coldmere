# Chapter 3: The Room Not on the Plan

I woke with the lamp gone to embers and the cold sitting on my chest, and the first thing I knew was that I hadn't dreamed the tone. The line in the hall was quiet now. But the listening quiet of it had followed me down into sleep and sat there, and I lay in the chair and looked at the gray square of the window and decided I was finished pretending the night would let me rest.

It was the small hours. The wind had eased. I trimmed the lamp up and the flame steadied, and I sat with my hands round the warm glass of it and made my decision the way I make all the difficult ones, which is to name the task and then do the task.

Upstairs. I hadn't done the upstairs.

My rule said morning. But the rule was made for houses that let you sleep, and this one wouldn't, and I would rather climb the dark with a lamp in my hand than lie under it waiting for a tone that never came twice. So I stood, and my knees told me the chair had cost me, and I took the lamp off the table and went out into the hall where the telephone sat black and silent on its table and didn't so much as gleam.

The stairs were the kind that announce you. I went up slow, lamp first, counting the treads under my breath because the count is a hand on the shoulder and I needed one. Eleven treads. A turn at a half-landing, the lamp throwing my shadow round the corner ahead of me so that I climbed toward it. Then four more to the landing, and the landing was wider than the rooms it served, a long bare run of board with doors set into it, and the cold up here was the same cold as below, a flat unbroken chill that had been in the house for weeks.

I stood on the landing and let my eyes do their work.

Doors. I counted them, because that was the trouble downstairs and the trouble would be up here too if it was anywhere. A door at each end. Two along the inner wall. I had the plan rolled in my coat and I took it out and held it to the lamp, and the upstairs was marked the way the down had been, in the bank's clean hand.

Two bedrooms front. A bathroom. A box room over the kitchen. A linen press. Five spaces, five doors, and I counted five doors on the landing and for a moment the arithmetic held and I was grateful for it.

Then I felt the warmth.

It came from the inner wall, between the bathroom door and the box-room door, from a stretch of plain papered wall where the plan drew nothing at all. It wasn't much. It was the warmth you feel passing an airing cupboard, the faint animal heat of a place that is somehow holding the cold off, and in a house where every other surface drank the chill straight out of me, it stopped me where I stood.

I put my hand flat to the wall and the wall was warm.

Not the warmth of a pipe. I know pipes. A pipe gives a line of heat, a seam you can trace. This was a broad gentle warmth across the whole face of the paper, as though a room behind it were lit and lived in, and I stood with my palm on the cold house's one warm wall and felt the small hairs go up on my arm.

There was a door.

I hadn't seen it because there was nothing to see, no frame proud of the wall, no handle, only a seam in the paper where a seam shouldn't be, a tall narrow rectangle that the eye slid off. I found the edge of it with my fingers. I pushed. It gave inward without a catch or a key, swinging on a hinge I never heard, and the warmth came out to meet me like breath.

The room beyond was small. A reading chair faced a single window. There was a footstool, and a low table with a lamp on it, unlit, and the window looked out and down across the dark to where I knew the pond lay, though I couldn't see it for the black. That was all. A chair, a stool, a table, a window. And the warmth, which had no source I could find, no fire, no radiator, no run of pipe, only the air of the place holding a blood-warm heat in the middle of a frozen house.

I stood in the doorway and didn't go in, and I did the thing I am for. I made it an inventory.

Reading chair, wingback, upholstered, the fabric a dull green worn pale on the arms. Condition: sound, much used. Footstool to match. Side table, mahogany, a reading lamp upon it. One window, single sash, curtains drawn back. I logged it in my head, lot by lot, because the logging is the railing and I held the railing, and the railing held until I looked at the chair.

The chair was occupied.

Not by a person. By the shape of one. There was a dent in the cushion, the deep settled hollow a body leaves over years of the same body in the same seat, and the same press in the back where shoulders had rested, and the green worn pale exactly where two forearms would lie.

The agent had told me she was found in this house, seated. He hadn't said where. I understood, standing in that warm doorway, that I was looking at where.

She had sat here. Facing the window. Facing the water she couldn't have seen in the dark any more than I could. And the house had kept the chair the shape of her.

I made myself cross the threshold. The warmth closed over me, and after the weeks of cold in my bones it was almost unbearable, a heat that didn't belong, that felt less like comfort than like being held. I went to the chair. I looked at the dent. And because I am thorough, because thoroughness is the only thing I have ever been able to put between myself and the dark, I pressed it flat.

I put my hand on the cushion and pushed the settled dent out of it, smoothed the worn green level, plumping it flat so the lot would photograph clean. The foam was cold to the touch, colder than the warm air around it, and it rose under my hand reluctant and slow, and then it was flat. An ordinary flat cushion in an ordinary chair.

I stepped back to be sure of it, and the dent came back.

I watched it come. It wasn't a settling, not the slow creep of old foam finding its memory. It pressed. The cushion went down under a weight that wasn't there, the deep body-dent reforming in the green, the shoulders in the back, the two pale worn places where forearms lay, and it happened in the time it took me to take one step away and turn, and the chair sat occupied again by a shape with no one in it, facing the window, facing the water.

I didn't move for a while. I want to be honest about that. I stood with the lamp and watched a chair hold the shape of a woman who was dead and removed from this house, and the warmth of the room lay on me like a hand, and the count came up in me without my calling it, the way it does. One chair. One window. One dent.

The plan. I had the plan in my other hand and I made myself look at it, because the plan was a fact and I needed a fact. Between the bathroom and the box room the plan drew solid wall. No door. No room. I took the lamp to the window and looked at where the curtain edge met the frame, and I looked at the two walls of the small room, and I knew, with the part of me that paces a house for a living, that this room sat in the yard of depth I couldn't account for downstairs. The yard the house ate and didn't give back. It was here. It was warm. And it wasn't on the plan, and it wasn't, I was already certain, in any photograph the bank or the agent or the dead woman herself had ever taken of this house.

A room can be left off a plan. Plans are made by tired men in a hurry, and a small room behind a papered door is easy to miss. I told myself that, standing in the warmth with the dead chair behind me. I told myself the way I had told myself about the cupboard and the tone, plainly, firmly, holding the railing. And the railing held, because it always holds, because I have spent thirty years making sure it holds.

But I noted it. I don't leave a thing off the page because the page frightens me. I took out my pencil and turned the plan over to the blank back, where there was room to write what the front had no line for, and I wrote: room off landing, inner wall, betw. bath & box rm. Not on plan. Then, because exactness is the whole of what I am, I wrote the rest. Warm. No source. Her chair. And then the thing I least wanted to write, the thing I wrote anyway, because the pencil is how I stay the person holding it and not the thing it describes.

Cushion holds the shape. I pressed it flat. It came back.

I underlined it once. The underlining didn't steady me the way it had downstairs. I stood in the only warm room in a freezing house, with a chair behind me settling under the press of nobody, and the lamp shaking gold across the green, and I looked at the dark window where a dead woman had sat looking down at water she couldn't see, and I made myself turn and go, and I left the door of that room standing open behind me because I couldn't make my hand close it.

The landing was cold. The cold was almost a relief.
