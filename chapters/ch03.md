# Chapter 3: The Room Not on the Plan

Sleep didn't come, so at last I took the torch to the stairs.

It was past midnight by the long-case clock's stopped face, which told me nothing, but my own watch said gone twelve and the house had settled into the deepest part of its quiet. I'd lain in the cold and listened to it and learned nothing, and a job half-walked sits worse with me than a strange staircase in the dark. The upstairs was tomorrow's work. I went up to make it today's.

The stairs were sound. Treads firm, no give, no woodworm in the rail under my hand. The landing opened at the top, and the plan was right about it: wider than the rooms it served, a long run of bare board with doors set down one side.

I counted them by torchlight. A bathroom, the door ajar, the cold coming off the tiles. Two bedrooms, both stripped to the bedframes, the air in them flat and old. A box room, a press of empty shelving.

Four doors. The plan said four. The count closed, and I stood a moment with it closing, which after the ground floor was a small mercy.

Then I turned, and there was a fifth door.

It sat at the end of the landing, past the box room, in a stretch of wall the plan drew as solid. I went back to the head of the stairs and held the torch to the plan to be sure. Solid wall. No door, no room, nothing but the exterior line and the run of the landing ending in a corner. I walked the length of it again with my hand on the plaster, counting, and the door was there under my hand where the page said wall.

A misdrawn plan. I'd told myself that downstairs and I told myself it here. But the cupboard downstairs had been a cupboard the plan miscounted as a room. This was the opposite. A room the plan had left off entirely, as if the surveyor had walked the landing and not seen the door at the end of it.

I put my hand to the latch. It was warm.

Not the warmth of a hand just lifted from it. The warmth of the latch itself, the iron of it holding heat in a house where every other latch I'd touched had been cold as the air. I stood with my fingers on it and felt the wrongness of that before I let myself name it.

I opened the door.

The heat came out to meet me. It was the only warm room in the house. The cold I'd carried up the stairs stopped at the threshold like a thing turned back, and beyond it the air was close and still and blood-warm, the warmth of a room lived in, a room with a fire lately in it, except there was no fire and no grate and the warmth had no source I could find.

I stood in the doorway and put the torch round the room. Small. Smaller than the bedrooms, a single window in the far wall. Under the window, a chair. A reading chair, wing-backed, the upholstery worn pale on the arms, turned to face the glass.

The window looked down on the pond. Even in the dark I could make out the shape of it below, the dull skin of ice catching what little light there was, and the chair set square to it, as if whoever sat here sat to watch the water.

Edith had been found in this room. The bank's file had said the warm room, and I'd read it as the front room or the kitchen, somewhere a person sits. She'd been found in a chair. This chair, turned to this window, looking down at this water.

There was a dent in the cushion. The pale worn seat held the shape of someone, pressed deep, the way a long-used chair holds the print of its owner. I went to it and laid my hand flat on the cushion. The warmth was in that too.

I don't know why I sat. To rest the count, maybe, or because the chair drew me the way a fire draws you in the cold. I sat, and the dent took me, and the window held the pond.

Then I stood, and turned to go.

The dent was there in the cushion. Pressed deep, the shape of someone, as if I had never sat at all.
