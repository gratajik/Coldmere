# Chapter 10: No Signal

By morning I'd decided to go. I sat over the cold range with the marbles in my pocket and the notebook shut on the stairs and the warm wall, and I weighed it like a job that's gone wrong. Some lots you don't finish. Some houses you leave half-cleared and write the office a line and let the next person have them.

I've done it twice in thirty years. Both times I knew the morning I knew it, and I knew it now.

The draft could wait. The draft was due Monday and the office could have it late or have it never. I'd drive to Harrow's Cross, ring them from the call box outside the post office, tell them the house wanted two pairs of hands and a man for the heavy work, and that was true, and let them send whom they liked.

I'd sleep in my own bed. The decision sat easy in me. I packed my bag while the light came up gray over the pond, and I didn't look out at the water while I did it.

I carried the bag to the front door and set it on the step and went back for the lamp, and at the door I turned the brass handle and the cold came in off the drive, and that was the first ordinary thing in two days. A drive. A car on it. Eleven miles of single track to a town with a call box and people in it.

The car stood where I'd left it, the windscreen furred with frost. I put the bag in the boot and got in and turned the key.

Nothing.

Not the cough of a tired engine, not the labored grind of a flat battery. Nothing. The key turned in the barrel and met no answer, the dash dead, no tick, no light, the starter not even reaching for the flywheel. I turned it again. I'm not a woman who panics at a car. A car's a box of parts and most of what goes wrong with one in the cold is the battery, and a battery's a known thing.

I got out and put up the bonnet and looked. I know an engine enough to find the terminals, and the terminals were clean and the leads were on and the battery was where it should be, and nothing was wet, nothing was cracked, nothing was loose. There was no reason in the engine I could see. The car had brought me down Fen Lane two days ago without a stutter and now it wouldn't wake.

I tried it twice more. The starter didn't catch. I took the key out and held it in my fist and didn't turn it a fifth time, because a fifth time would've been a thing I did past reason, and I wouldn't be that yet.

So. The car was a fact and the fact was no. I'm a woman who works with what's in front of her. There was the phone.

I went back into the house with the cold sitting on me and I didn't let myself think about the warm wall upstairs being the one place in eleven miles with any warmth in it. I went to the hall table and lifted the black receiver off the rotary phone, and I put it to my ear, and I waited for the tone.

The tone came up slow.

It came up like it had the first night and every time since, a held half-second of quiet first, a stretch of nothing on the line where a working line gives you the note at once, and then the dial tone, flat and steady, after. I'd told myself old copper. I'd told myself a tired exchange. I stood with the receiver to my ear and the slow tone going in it and I didn't believe old copper anymore.

I dialed the office. I knew the number from the night before and I dialed it with my finger in the cold holes of the dial, and the dial whirred back, and the line did the thing a calling line does, the click, the open space, and then it rang.

It rang. I let it ring. I counted the rings, One, Two, in my own habit, Three, Four, and on the fourth ring it should have answered, it had answered on the fourth ring the morning before, and it rang past four, Five, Six, into the empty after-hours of a daytime office that had no business being empty at this hour. Seven. Eight.

I put the receiver down. It wasn't after-hours. It was morning, a working morning, and the office answered its phone of a working morning, and it had answered yesterday, and now it didn't.

I lifted the receiver again and dialed the operator, the three nines, the number you dial when the ordinary numbers fail. The dial whirred back. The line opened on the slow quiet, the held half-second, and then nothing happened. No ring. No voice. Just the open line, going on, like it had gone on near the bottom of sleep that first night with no hand to put it there, a line that was open to something and answering late.

"Hello," I said into it.

The held quiet held. Then, after the half-second, the line gave back the faint sound of a line with someone on it. Not a voice. The small breathing space of an open connection, present, listening, a half-step behind, as if whatever was at the other end had heard me and was taking its time to know it.

I put the receiver down. I put it down without a word and I stepped back from the table and I stood in the cold hall with my hands at my sides and I didn't pick it up again.

I got out my mobile then. I hadn't looked at it since the cattle-grid, since three bars went to none and the screen said No service, and I looked at it now in the gray light of the hall with the lamp burning beside me. No service. I held it up.

I walked it to the front door and held it on the step in the open air and turned it slowly the way you do, hunting the one inch of sky where a signal might lie. The bars stayed flat. No service. I'd lost it at the third grid two days back and I hadn't had it since, and I wouldn't have it here.

So that was the whole of it. The car was no. The landline gave a slow tone and an office that wouldn't answer and an operator that didn't come and an open line with something on it. The mobile was no. I stood on the step with the cold dead phone in my hand and the dead car in the drive and the empty lane going up past three cattle-grids and a bridge to a town eleven miles off, and I understood that I was a woman who couldn't get word out and couldn't get herself out either.

I could walk it. The thought came and I held it up against the morning. Eleven miles of single track in late November, three grids and a bridge, the light such as it was, and the cold. I've walked further. I'm not soft.

I stood on the step and I made myself reckon it honestly, the miles, the cold, the dark that would come down before I was halfway, and the truth under the reckoning, which was that walking out meant walking down the drive, past the pond, with my back to the house and the water at my side for the first eighty yards, in the open, alone, on foot. And I couldn't. I stood on the step and looked at the lane and I couldn't make my feet go to it, not past the water, not on foot, not yet.

I went back inside and shut the door.

I shut it myself, and the house didn't lock me in. There was no lock turned, no door barred, no chain across. The car wouldn't start and that's a car. The phone gave a slow tone and that's old copper.

The mobile had no signal and that's eleven miles of country with no mast. The lane was open. The door was on a latch I could lift. Every fact of it had an honest name, and I could reach for the name, and the names were all true, and they didn't change the one thing the facts came to, which was that I'd decided to go and I wasn't going.

The house hadn't laid a hand on me. Every fact of it had an honest name, and the names held me anyway.

I stayed in the cold hall with the lamp going and the marbles in my pocket and the dead phone behind me and I felt the count somewhere above me in the wall, not sounding, but there, patient as a thing that waits, even, with all its order, for the night and the quiet to go on with what it was counting. I didn't write any of it down. I had the notebook and the pencil and I didn't take them up, because there was no column for a woman who could leave and wouldn't, and writing it wouldn't make a column, and I was tired of writing things that didn't make a column.

I carried the bag back up from the step and set it by the kitchen table, not unpacked, ready, a small lie I told myself, that I was still going, that this was a held departure and not a stopped one. I sat down in the kitchen chair where I'd slept two nights and I put my hands flat on the cold table and I looked at the gray light coming up over the pond through the window I wouldn't go nearer, and I waited, and I didn't know what for, and the slow tone in the hall, when I listened for it, was no longer the line.
