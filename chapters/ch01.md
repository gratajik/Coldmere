# Chapter 1: The Inventory

The bank's letter called it a property. I called it a clearance. Eleven miles down Fen Lane, past three cattle-grids and one culvert bridge, the road ran out at a gate, and beyond the gate Coldmere sat in the last of the light like something set down and forgotten.

I parked on the gravel and didn't get out at once. I read the file again, though I knew it. Edith Vane, deceased. No will located. Contents to be inventoried, then sold at the bank's reserve. My name on the work order because I was the only Vane relation who answered the phone, and because emptying the dead is what I am paid to do.

I got out. The cold came up off the ground through my boots. Late November, the grass already stiff, the sky bleeding out behind the chimneys.

The drive curved past the house's flank, and from there I saw the water. A mill-pond, downslope, perhaps eighty yards over rough ground, low and dull and skinned at the edges with the first ice. I looked at it the length of one breath. Then I looked at the clipboard instead, which is what I am for.

Condition: the front of the house was sound. Slate roof, no obvious slip. Render cracked at the lintels, foxing of damp under the gutters, sash windows original and tight. I noted it. A house is a box of facts, and I had come to count them.

The key turned hard. Inside, the hall held the same cold as the yard, only stiller, the kind that has settled in over years and made itself at home. Tiled floor. A coat stand, empty. A long-case clock, stopped. I switched on the lights and half of them came up thin and brown, the wiring old, the power barely making the journey.

I work to a method, and the method is the only thing I trust. I walk the ground floor first and square it against the plan. Then I tag, room by room, lot by lot.

Condition, then number, then a seal where a seal is wanted. The dead don't argue with a manifest. That is the comfort of the work.

The bank had sent a floor plan, drawn up for the sale. I unfolded it on the hall table and held it down with my torch. Six rooms on the ground floor, the plan said, off a central passage. Hall, study, two reception rooms, kitchen, scullery.

I counted them by hand, walking the passage with the plan held against the wall.

Study. One.

Reception. Two.

The second reception. Three.

Kitchen. Four. Scullery, off the kitchen. Five.

I came back to the hall and stood with the plan and the count not closing. Five rooms walked. Six on the page. I went round again, slower, opening each door and shutting it, the way you check a column of figures when the total is wrong. Study. Two receptions. Kitchen. Scullery. Five.

There was a door under the stairs I hadn't opened. I opened it. A broom cupboard, shallow, a single shelf, nothing that counted as a room. I shut it and made it the sixth anyway, because the plan wanted six and a cupboard was the only candidate, and because a misdrawn plan is the dullest explanation there is. Surveyors round up. They count a pantry, or a porch, or they simply miscount, and the work order inherits the error. I have cleared a hundred houses whose plans lied in small ways.

I logged it. Six rooms, one of them a cupboard the plan called a room. Then I underlined the discrepancy, the way I underline anything that doesn't yet sit flat, and moved on. It was a number that wouldn't close, and I do not leave a number that will not close. I leave a note that says so.

The hall had gone fully dark while I walked. I lit the torch properly and stood it on the table, beam to the ceiling, and the light pooled and left the stairs in shadow. Upstairs there would be more of the same: bedrooms, a bathroom, a landing wider, the plan said, than the rooms it served. Lots, conditions, seals. Tomorrow's work.

I didn't go up. I told myself it was the light, the wiring too thin to trust on a strange staircase in the dark. I'm not afraid of empty houses. I empty them.

I set my bag at the foot of the stairs and turned the torch toward the front room where I would sleep. Behind me, through the cold glass, the pond held the last grey light and gave none of it back.
