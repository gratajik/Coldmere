# Chapter 6: A Childless House

Past noon I worked back through the house, room by room, and came to a door at the end of the ground-floor passage I'd marked on my first walk as a back bedroom. A servant's room, by its size and place. I'd left it for the afternoon. I opened it with a tag in my hand and the lot list filling, and stood in the doorway and didn't write anything down for a while.

It was a child's room.

A small bed against the wall. Not a cot, not a crib, a proper bed but built short, the frame turned narrow and low, the kind made for a body that hasn't its full length yet.

I went to it and pressed the mattress with the flat of my hand the way I'd pressed every mattress in the house. Sound. Frame sound, no woodworm in the rails. I lifted the cover. A blanket folded square at the bed's end, wool, moth-holed but whole.

The room had been kept. That was the first thing my eye gave me. The bedrooms upstairs were stripped to their frames, the studies and receptions long gone over, but this room held its things the way a room holds them when no one has the heart to clear it.

On a low shelf by the window stood a tin. I took it down. A biscuit tin, the lid rusted at the seam, and inside, when I worked it off, marbles. A double handful of them, glass, the colours gone cloudy with age.

I rolled them under my thumb. Cat's-eyes, and clearies, and one larger steelie heavy as a ball-bearing. I'd had a tin like it once. Every child had.

I set it down on the bed and opened the press in the corner.

Clothes. Boy's clothes, small, hung on wire hangers and folded on the shelf below. Short trousers, grey flannel. Shirts with the collars gone soft. A jumper, knitted, navy, the cuffs let down once and the let-down gone short again. I took the jumper off its hanger and held it up, and it was the size of a boy of seven, or near it, the chest of it no wider than my two spread hands.

I put it back. I am precise about putting things back.

I'd planted myself in the middle of the room with the tin on the bed and the press open and the short bed against the wall, and I did the only thing the room left me, which was to take out the inventory and try to lot it.

The bed, lot. Child's, short frame, sound. The tin and its marbles, lot. The press and what it held, lot. I wrote the lines, and the lines were correct, condition and quantity, the way every line I have ever written is correct.

But a lot is a thing accounted for, and these were not accounted for. Edith never married. The bank's file said it twice, in two hands, no spouse, no issue. No child was born in this house. I had walked every room of it and there was nowhere a family had been, no second wardrobe, no grown man's coat, no woman's things but Edith's own in the room she died in. A childless house. And one room at the back of it kept for a boy of seven, the bed made up, the clothes hung, the marbles in their tin on the shelf.

I have cleared houses that grief had stopped the clock in. A nursery left as it was, a room a mother couldn't enter. I know that room. It has a date you can find, a birth and a small grave, a story the neighbours tell you at the gate. This had none. No date, no grave, no boy on any paper. Only the things.

I closed the press. I picked up the tin and turned it in my hands, the marbles shifting and clicking inside, and I had a thought I didn't write down, the way I hadn't written down the count climbing to a total: that a thing kept this carefully is not a thing left behind. It is a thing held.

I put the tin back on the shelf where it had stood. I straightened the blanket I'd lifted, folded it square again where it had lain on the short bed.

Then I went out and pulled the door to behind me, and waited in the cold of the passage with my pen in my hand and my lot list filled, and the one room in the house I couldn't rule a line under at my back.
