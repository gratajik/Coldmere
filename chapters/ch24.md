# Chapter 24: The Door

Eighty yards is nothing on two feet in summer. On hands and knees in the wet of a frost morning it is a manifest of its own, item by item, each yard a thing to be got up and over and set behind me.

I went up the slope the only way that was left, palms flat on the ice-crust where the ground had frozen, knees driving, the soaked wool of my coat already stiffening to a shell. My hands had stopped being hands. I knew the fire had cooked them at the tips because I had felt it happen, but I could not feel them now, only a far-off report that they were down on the ground and bearing weight, like a thing felt through a glove two sizes wrong.

The slope was a wall.

I reckoned the yards because a thing reckoned is a thing you can finish. I got to ten and lost it and started again and lost that too, and after a while I stopped reaching for the number and only moved, one knee, one knee, the next ridge, the next.

The house stood at the top of it, blacker than the dark, one window grey where the morning was thinning. The front door was a paler oblong against the black, and it was not flush in its frame. It stood a hand's width open.

I fixed on the gap.

There is a thing you do at the close of a long clearance, when the rooms are emptied and the lots are sealed and there is one item left and you are too tired to lift it: you look at it, and you make your hands do the thing your hands no longer want to do, because the job is not done until that item is moved. The door was what remained. I crawled toward it.

Partway up, the cold finished climbing me and reached the place it had been climbing toward. The shaking stopped. That was the worst of it. I had been shaking from the pond, the deep racking shake of a body fighting the cold, and on the slope the shake went out of me and a warmth came up in its place, a false warmth, soft and full, that wanted me to lie down in the frozen ground and rest a moment, only a moment, the same warmth as the room above with the chair in it.

I knew that warmth. I had felt its weight before. It was not in the air. It was in me, a body shutting its own shutters against the cold, and it was lying to me.

I did not lie down. I drove the next knee.

The slope eased near the top and I came onto the level of the drive, the gravel furred white, the dead car a black bulk to my left with its frosted glass. I got past it. The step was three yards on. I could see the brass door plate grey-dark, the worn shine where a thumb had pressed it a hundred years.

The cold came through the gap in the door to meet me. There was no warmth out of the house. The hall behind the open door was as black and dead as the hall I had crawled out of an hour before, colder if anything, the November chill of every room in Coldmere pouring out the hand's-width gap into the colder morning. The lamp I had left in the study had burned down to nothing. The hall was dark.

But the door stood open. I had shut it behind me going down. I knew I had. I had hauled it to with the ledgers in my arms and heard the latch take. Now it stood a hand's width back from its frame, the cold streaming out of it, as if the house had set it ajar after me and left it so, ready, the way you leave a door for a thing you are expecting and do not want to have to get up and let in.

I got my hand up to the gap. I could not feel the edge of the door. I watched my hand close on the dark line of it and willed the fingers to bend, and they bent slow, late, like the line giving back its tone a half-second after you lift the receiver, and the door gave under a hand I could not feel pushing it.

It swung in on its silent hinge.

The dark hall opened ahead of me. No light. No flame. No even glow from a room just stepped out of. Only the black of the rooms I knew, the stairs going up into worse black, and somewhere at the top of them, a long way up the eleven treads, the one warm room with the chair in it and the dent that fills.

The cold of the open morning was at my back. The cold of the dead house was at my face. The warmth was in neither. It was only in me, the soft full false warmth of a body giving out, and I knelt on the threshold with one hand on the open door and understood what it was, and what it meant that the house had left the door standing open.

The chair upstairs warms when a name has just gone in. The room warms when a name has just gone in. There was no warmth come down to the door, because no name had gone in yet. The door was only open. The door was open because the house was ready, and the keeping does not need to come down the stairs to meet a thing it has already gotten cold enough to want to come in.

I did not go in.

I want this plain, and the catalog has the column for it, which is the only mercy left to me: I knelt at the threshold of the open house with the cold morning behind me and I did not cross. To cross was to come in out of the wind, into the lee of the walls, where a body stops losing heat to the open. The cold outside was killing me faster. The body that had stopped shaking and gone soft and full wanted nothing in the world but the dark dry hall, the door shut against the wind, the long cold rooms that were warmer than this.

I had crawled eighty yards up a frozen slope to reach this door because the door was out. And I knelt on the sill of it and saw that the open dark hall on the other side was not out. It was the deepest in there was.

The register was ash and ruin in the pond behind me. I had burned it believing the boy would come loose, believing a thing burned is written nowhere. I knelt at the door and felt the warmth in me, the false warmth of my own body shutting down, and knew, like the tape knowing a joint, that the burning had not stopped the house from setting its door open after me.

The book was ash and the keeping was not. The keeping had left the door ajar. It had set the latch back a hand's width and waited in the cold dark for the thing it had been working toward to crawl in out of the killing frost, the way a man leaves the door on the latch for the car he has been waiting for, no need to get up, no need to come out to the step, only the gap left open and the certainty that the cold will do the rest.

The open door wanted me to come in.

I knelt on the sill between the cold that was killing me and the dark that was worse than the cold, and the choosing was not the choosing I had thought I crawled up to make. I had thought the door was the choice between dying on the slope and living in the house. It wasn't. The slope behind me was the open cold, and the open cold was the end of me, plain, a body giving out in a frost.

The hall ahead was the lee of the walls, and the lee of the walls was the chair, and the room, and the dent that refills, and a name that goes in once you are in out of the wind and the warmth finds you at last. To go in was not to live. To go in was to be taken in. And being taken in, in this house, is what the kept are.

I took my hand off the door.

It hung where it was, open on the dark, the cold streaming out, the black hall holding itself ready for the one it had been left ajar for. I knelt and watched it stand open and did not crawl into the lee of it. The body wanted the shelter the way the slope pulls a thing down to its low point.

I held against the pull the one way I had left, which was not strength, there was no strength in me, but only the refusal that had got me down the slope and through the fire, the same refusal I had set against the thing in the kitchen doorway wearing my brother's face. I would not be taken in. I turned my own face from the open dark as I had turned it from his.

I got my hand back down onto the cold gravel of the step, on the outside of the sill, in the wind, in the killing cold, and I held it there, palm flat on the frozen stone, while the open door stood dark and ready behind me and waited for me to come in out of the cold.

The grey at the one window had spread. First light was coming up over the slope and the broken place a mile off where the flood still poured easy through the gap. The frost was on everything, the drive, the dead car, the worn brass plate, my own sleeve glazed with ice. The cold went into me the final stretch and the door behind me stood open on the dark, patient, the latch back a hand's width, and I knelt on the threshold with my burned hand flat on the freezing step and did not go in, and could not go on, and the morning came up grey over the pond that keeps what it takes.
