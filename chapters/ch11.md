# Chapter 11: The Figure at the Water

Sleep took me near three and gave me back near four. I knew the time without a clock by the dead-gray cast at the kitchen window, the hour the dark begins to thin and the cold sits heaviest. The lamp had gone to its low bead. I sat up in the chair with my coat slid off one shoulder and my neck stiff, and for a held moment I didn't know which house I was in, and then I did.

The bag stood by the table where I'd set it. Ready. The small lie of it.

I got up to turn the lamp higher, because that is what I do at this hour, I add light. I carried it through to the hall to look at the front door I'd shut, to be sure it was shut, which was foolish, I'd shut it myself, but a woman wakes in a cold house and counts her exits. The door was shut. The latch lay in its keep. I put my hand on the cold brass and it was only brass.

I should have gone back to the chair.

I went instead to the foot of the stairs, carrying the light, because the warm room was up there and the count was up there and a thing in me that I do not name wanted to know if either was awake. The stairwell held its dark. The treads went up dry. I waited at the bottom tread with one foot on it and listened, and there was no count, only the house breathing its weeks-old cold, and I might have gone up, I felt the pull of it, the warm wall and the chair and the dent.

The landing window faced the wrong way. The pull was up. I went, against it, to the drawing-room window instead, the long one that gives onto the slope, because that window faces the water and I hadn't let myself look at the water in two days, and at near four in the morning, with no sleep in me, the not-looking had worn thin.

I drew the curtain back an inch.

The slope went down gray to the black line of the trees. Below the trees, at the bottom of the rough ground, the pond lay under its thin skin of ice, paler than the dark around it, a flat dull coin of frozen water perhaps eighty yards off. I'd glimpsed it from the drive my first dusk and locked it away. I made myself look at it now, the whole low oval of it, the reeds gone to black stubble at the near edge, the ice catching what little light the sky let down.

A small shape stood at the edge.

I want to set it down exactly. At the near edge of the pond, on the rough black ground where the reeds gave out, a shape stood. It was the height of a child. It faced the house. It did not move.

I held the curtain and I didn't breathe and I counted, because the count is the railing. One. The shape, dark against the paler ice. Two. Its head, its shoulders, the narrow trunk of it, child-sized, six or seven years to the height of it. Three. Facing up the slope, facing the window, facing me. Four. Not moving. Five. Still not.

A deer. The word came up and I took hold of it the way a drowning hand takes a rope. A young deer come down to the water, a fawn at the reeds, and the dark and the hour making a shape of it. I've stood at a hundred dark windows in a hundred dead houses and made men of coat-stands and faces of damp on plaster. The eye wants a shape and finds one. A deer.

But a deer stands four-legged and low. This stood upright, on two, narrow, with the held stillness of a thing that has stopped on purpose and is waiting. A deer would have moved. A deer would have bent its head to the ice, or startled, or drifted off into the trees. This didn't bend. This didn't drift. It stood at the place where the water meets the land and it kept its face to the house through the long minutes I watched, and I watched a long time, longer than the watching took, because I couldn't make myself stop.

A trespasser, then. A child from a farm, lost, wandered eleven miles down a single track in late November in the black before dawn to stand at a frozen pond and face an empty house. I heard the reasoning as I made it and the reasoning was thin as the ice.

There was no farm within eleven miles. There was no child to come down the lane. The lane was open and the lane brought no one. I'd tried that lane myself this morning and it had given me nothing.

Three reasons, three ropes, and every one of them rotted in my hand.

I could go down. The thought came flat and terrible. I could take the lamp and go out the front door I'd shut with my own hand and down the drive and across the rough ground the eighty yards to the water and see what stood there, see it close, name it, end it, because naming things and ending them is the whole of what I do, I am a woman who walks up to the thing and writes down what it is. I had my hand on the curtain and the door was twelve feet behind me and the shape stood at the water and waited, and waiting is what a thing does that wants you to come.

I didn't go down.

I'll set that against thirty years too. I waited at the window with the lamp throwing my own dim shape back at me off the glass, and below me on the ice-edge the small still figure faced the house, and the whole of my trade said go and see, and I couldn't make my feet move toward the water. Not toward the pond. Not in the dark. Not down the slope where the ground falls away to the place the reeds give out and the water begins.

Because I knew the height of it.

I had measured that height with my own eyes two days running. The white iron bed at the dead end of the passage was that long. The grey flannel shorts in the drawer were cut for a child of that height. The wet prints on the fourth tread and the seventh were the foot of a child of that height, climbing. And now a child of exactly that height stood at the exact edge of the exact water where, thirty years ago, on a bright summer afternoon, my brother had walked in and not come out, and I had looked away.

There. That is the lid up. That is the drawer open and the thing out of it and I can't put it back.

I had looked away. That was the fact under all the other facts, the one I had carried down Fen Lane and into this cold house and hadn't let myself touch. He was seven. He had the red marble in his fist, the red one, he always took the red one. I was nine and I was meant to be watching him and I looked away, and when I looked back the water was closing, just closing, the rings going out flat to the reeds, and I screamed and they came and they dragged the pond for two days and brought up weed and a boot and a bicycle frame and not my brother. Never my brother.

And now a small shape his exact height stood at the water in the dark and faced the house, and waited, and the wanting in me to go down and the terror of going down were the same size. That is the thing I can't explain, that I wanted to go to it as much as I have ever wanted anything, that some part of me would have gone into the pond after it gladly. I shook against the glass and held.

I let the curtain fall.

I kept my back to the window in the dark drawing room and I didn't look again. I want that on the record. I let the curtain down and I turned to the cold hearth, the small flame held between my two hands, shaking, and I didn't draw the curtain back to see if the figure was still there, because if it was still there I would have to keep not going, all night, and if it was gone that was worse, that was a thing that had come to the edge of the water and stood facing the house and then walked back into the dark toward wherever it bides, and I couldn't bear either, and so I didn't look.

I went back to the kitchen and I sat in the chair. I didn't take up the pencil. There is no column for a woman who saw her dead brother's height at the water and wouldn't go down. I have logged the count and the prints and the ball. I didn't log this.

I sat with the marbles cold against my leg, nineteen of them, one red, and I held the lamp in my lap like a thing that might keep me warm, and the gray came up slow at the window, and somewhere below the trees the water lay under its skin of ice with whatever stood at the edge of it, and I sat with my back to the dark and waited for a morning I had no reason left to think would help me.
