# Chapter 5: Cataloging the Dead

Morning gave me back the house as a job, and a job I can do.

The counting had stopped sometime before light. I woke to a grey window and a cold room and the plain fact of work to be done, and I was glad of it. A house full of facts. Six rooms downstairs, one a cupboard, and an inventory in my bag waiting for the lots to fill it. I made my tea on the camp stove, ate standing up, and went to work in the study first, because the study was nearest and I start where I stand.

This is the part I am good at. Walk the room. Read it left to right, top to bottom, the way you read a wall of stock before a sale.

A house tells you what it was by what it kept, and you write that down without minding it.

The study had a desk, a swivel chair, two cabinets, and a glass-fronted case I left for later. I tagged the desk first. Walnut, veneer lifting at the front edge, one drawer-runner gone. Condition noted, lot number written on the buff card, the card looped to the handle with string. Lot one. I tied it and moved on.

The chair, lot two. The cabinets, three and four. I work fast and I work clean, and the rhythm of it is the nearest thing I have to rest. Tag, condition, lot. Tag, condition, lot. The cards went on one after another and the room emptied of its anonymity room by room, each thing made a known quantity, a line I could rule under.

I crated the small stuff as I went. That is the method. Anything loose and saleable goes into a tea-chest, and when the chest is full I seal it, label the seal, and log its contents on the manifest, so that nothing leaves a house I have cleared that I haven't seen and named.

I am precise about the seal. A house in probate is a house in dispute, and a sealed crate is a crate no one can argue with later.

I cut my own paper tape from the roll, wet it, laid it over the lid, and signed across the join with the date, the way I always do, so that the join can't be opened without the signature tearing. I keep a stub of indelible pencil for it. The signature is mine and the date is the day, and a seal once signed is a fact I can stand behind.

I filled the first chest from the study shelves. Glassware, a clock without works, a box of letters I didn't read because reading them isn't the job. Sealed, signed, logged. Lot five, the chest itself. I stacked it by the door and started the second.

By mid-morning the study was bare boards and the long lines of my own neat cards, and I stood in the door of it and felt the small clean satisfaction I always feel: a room reduced to its inventory, a place made accountable. Nothing in this house is a mystery once it has a lot number. That is what I tell the families, the ones who can't bear to do it themselves, and it is true. You cannot grieve a manifest.

I didn't think about the counting. Or I thought about it the way you think about a sound a car made on the drive home, once, and not since. Pipes. The wind. A thing that doesn't survive daylight.

I moved into the front reception and began again. Tag, condition, lot. The sofa, lot nine, the springs gone but the frame sound. A sideboard, lot ten. I worked the room down to its corners and ruled my lines and tied my cards, and the morning went the way a good morning goes, which is to say it went without anything in it I couldn't name.

I sealed the third chest near noon. Cut the tape, wet it, laid it. Signed the join with the date and the indelible pencil, pressed hard, so the ink took.

Then I logged it on the manifest in my own hand, the contents listed and the seal described, the way I described every chest in every house I had ever emptied, and I had no reason yet to know that the manifest was the one document in Coldmere I would come to trust the least.
