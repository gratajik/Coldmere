# What the House Keeps

*A novella. Author: **Greg Ratajik**. Genre: literary gothic horror / haunted house. Length target: ~19,000 words, 25 chapters. First person, past tense, single POV (Iris Pell). The house is named **Coldmere**.*

## Premise

Iris Pell catalogs and clears the estates of the dead for a living. Condition reports, lot numbers, a clean inventory, then the auctioneers come. When her estranged great-aunt Edith Vane dies alone and the bank moves to seize the house, Iris is the only relative willing to drive the eleven miles down Fen Lane to empty Coldmere before the sale. She has not been to the house in thirty years, not since the summer her little brother Danny drowned in the mill-pond behind it and was never found.

Coldmere is wrong in small ways first. A room off the upstairs hall that appears on no floor plan and in no photograph, and that, alone in a freezing house, is blood-warm. A child's things in a house where no child was ever born. Wet, small footprints on the stairs that dry from the outside in. Someone counting in the walls, reciting numbers in the cadence of an inventory. And in Edith's meticulous ledgers, columns of names and dates going back over a century, a housemaid in 1911, a boy in 1948, others, and near the end, in Edith's own hand, Danny's name, dated the summer he died.

The house keeps its dead. It has kept Danny for thirty years. Edith was its keeper, and now Edith is kept. The count needs one more, and the last entry in the final ledger is already written, ahead of time, in Edith's hand: Iris's name, with a date a few days out.

## Themes

- Grief as the door the house walks through. The house does not invent Iris's guilt; it uses it.
- Inventory against the uncountable. Iris narrates in the language of cataloging, condition, provenance, lot, and the house slowly turns that language against her until she is an item in it.
- What "keeping" means: not rest, not peace. Possession. The dead here are held, not at rest.
- The unrecovered body. The particular horror of a loss with no proof and no grave, and what it means to learn the loss was a taking.

## Characters

### Iris Pell
Protagonist and narrator. Female. 38. Estate-clearance archivist; she empties dead people's houses for auction houses and banks. Practical, dry, grief-armored; she keeps the world at arm's length behind a clipboard. Thirty years ago, at nine, she looked away for a moment at the pond and her brother was gone; she has organized her whole life around never looking away again, which is why she catalogs the dead. She does not believe in ghosts. She believes in provenance. The house teaches her the difference. She refers to herself rarely; when she does, the pronoun stays *she/her* throughout. The house's corruption is in the prose, never in her gender. **Secret she carries:** that the moment she looked away she was watching her own reflection in the water, not Danny. **Wants:** to do one more clean job and leave. **Needs:** to stop standing guard over a death she could not have prevented. **Arc:** from cataloguer who masters the dead to an item the dead inventory.

### Danny Pell
Iris's younger brother. Male. Drowned in the mill-pond at seven, thirty years ago; body never recovered. The child the house has held since. What appears wearing him is not him, though it knows everything he knew. He/him. **Function:** the lure. The house's most precise instrument against Iris because it is the wound she never closed.

### Edith Vane
Iris's great-aunt, recently deceased. Female. Never married, no children. Found seated in the warm room, no cause of record. The house's keeper for fifty years, the one who wrote the ledgers, who fed the count, and who, at the end, was taken into it herself. She reads in the ledgers as exacting, complicit, and finally trapped. She/her. Appears only as a body, in photographs, in her handwriting, and once, terribly, in the warm room. **Secret:** she did not lure Danny to the pond out of malice; she was buying time, feeding the count to keep her own name out of it, and Danny was the price she chose. **Arc (in arrears, read out of the ledgers):** dutiful keeper, then complicit keeper, then kept.

### The kept
The dead the house holds. Named in the ledgers: **Annie Sewell**, housemaid (female, 1911); **Thomas Reed**, a boy (male, 1948); and others, sparser, older. They are not characters so much as a register. Danny is the most recent until Edith. They do not speak so much as count.

## Character Voice Cards

### Iris Pell (narrator, all chapters)
- **Average sentence length:** 9-16 words at rest; collapses to 3-7 under stress.
- **Vocabulary register:** technical-plain. The lexicon of estate clearance and condition reporting: *lot, provenance, condition, foxing, water-staining, seal, manifest, reserve, hammer price.* Literary only by precision, never by ornament.
- **Metaphor domain:** cataloging, auction, archival conservation, inventory. She measures the world in lots and conditions. Damage is described as a conservator describes it. When she reaches for a feeling she reaches first for a category.
- **Speech patterns:** declarative. States the object, then its condition, then files it. Counts to steady herself ("One window. Two. Three."). Resists the figurative; when a figure escapes her it signals the house is winning.
- **Stress response:** sentences shorten and fracture. She drops verbs. She retreats into the catalog ("Condition: held.") when she cannot bear the plain sentence. She does NOT run on; she clips.
- **Fragment vs. run-on:** fragment-leaning, hard. Never run-on.
- **Tells:** "Condition:" as an opener; "I logged it" / "I wrote it down anyway"; the steadying count; "provenance"; clipped repair-noun nouns.
- **Failure-mode anchors:**
  - GOOD: "The bed was small. Child-sized. Mattress sound, frame sound, no woodworm. I tagged it Lot 19 and stood in the doorway longer than the tagging took."
  - GOOD: "I do not believe in ghosts. I believe a house is a box of facts and I am paid to empty it."
  - DRONE (avoid): "Standing there in the cold of that strange room, I found myself reflecting on all the years I had spent running from a grief I could neither name nor lay down, a grief that had followed me from house to house like a debt I would never finish paying."
  - DRONE (avoid): "It was almost as if the house seemed to want something from me, as though some part of me had always known, in some buried place, that I would one day return."

### Edith Vane (ledger voice; appears in handwriting, photographs, once in the warm room)
- **Average sentence length:** 3-8 words. Clerical fragments. Name. Year. One-word condition.
- **Vocabulary register:** archaic-clerical, formal, uncontracted. *Settled. Kept on. Wanting. In hand. Discharged.*
- **Metaphor domain:** none on the page; the household account-book. Stock, ledger, balance, the running total. The dread is that it rhymes exactly with Iris's catalog voice.
- **Speech patterns:** entries, not sentences. "Annie Sewell. 1911. Settled." Where she breaks into prose (the late, fractured entries) she does so reluctantly, in short uncontracted lines that read like a woman writing against her own hand.
- **Register ratio (non-human-adjacent / compelled voice):** plain-clerical default (~75%); the few prose breaks where dread or compulsion surface use slightly raised, archaic phrasing (~25%), reserved for the late entries. No purple. No menace adjectives.
- **Stress response (the late entries):** the hand falters into qualification, then steadies into duty. "I write what I am given to write." Forbidden: lyric collapse, exclamation.
- **Forbidden steady-state patterns:** no anaphora loops; no regret-stack compound sentences. The keeper does not lament at length; she logs.
- **Required interest devices:** the clean horror of a household account applied to the dead; one-word conditions that accrue weight by repetition; the single line where competence cracks.
- **Tells:** "Settled."; "Kept on."; "Wanting one."; "In hand."; uncontracted forms throughout.
- **Failure-mode anchors:**
  - GOOD: "Thomas Reed. 1948. Kept on. The room was warm three nights."
  - GOOD: "The count is short by one. I have until the frost."
  - DRONE (avoid): "Oh, how I have suffered under the terrible weight of this house, how its cold halls have stolen every warmth from my long and lonely years."
  - DRONE (avoid): "It wanted, and wanted, and wanted, and I could not stop the wanting of it."

### The count / the house (non-human; counting in the walls)
- **Average sentence length:** 1-4 words. Bare numerals in auction cadence.
- **Vocabulary register:** the stock-take. Numbers. The occasional flat instruction in the cadence of a sale ("Lot forty. A pause. Lot forty-one.").
- **Metaphor domain:** none. The horror is the absence of metaphor: a mundane procedure, the inventory call, misapplied to the dead.
- **Register ratio (non-human voice):** modern-plain default (~90%); no archaic register at all. The count never lyricizes. It enumerates. Deep-formal is FORBIDDEN here; the dread is bureaucratic, not gothic-poetic.
- **Forbidden steady-state patterns:** no whispered secrets, no menace, no anaphora, no "it wanted" loops. Numerals and pauses only.
- **Required interest devices:** the climb of the count toward a single number; the realization that the number is a lot; auction-floor flatness where the reader expects threat.
- **Tells:** "Lot [number]."; "A pause."; bare ascending numerals.
- **Failure-mode anchors:**
  - GOOD: "Lot thirty-nine. A pause. Lot forty."
  - GOOD: "Three. Then four. Then, after the pipe-knock, five."
  - DRONE (avoid): "The voices rose in a terrible chorus of ancient hunger, whispering the secrets of the dead."
  - DRONE (avoid): "It counted, and in its counting was all the sorrow of the world."

### The thing that wears Danny (non-human; appears ch6 traces onward, embodied ch20)
- **Average sentence length:** 4-10 words. A child's plain speech, accurate and slightly behind.
- **Vocabulary register:** a seven-year-old's, circa thirty years ago. Period-accurate, concrete, no modern slang. It knows what Danny knew and produces it exactly, which is the wrong of it.
- **Metaphor domain:** none; child-literal. Marbles, the ball, the water, "you looked away."
- **Register ratio (non-human voice):** child-modern-plain default (~95%); no archaic register. The horror is fidelity, not eeriness. When it slips, it slips into the count's numerals, never into poetry.
- **Forbidden steady-state patterns:** no spectral hissing, no "come to me" cliché, no anaphora. It states facts Danny would have stated. Its single recurring move is to know one detail too many.
- **Required interest devices:** the unbearable accuracy (it names the marble Iris lost; it repeats the last thing Danny said); the half-second lag, like the dial tone; the line where it counts instead of answers.
- **Tells:** "You looked away."; repeating a true past detail; lapsing into a numeral mid-sentence.
- **Failure-mode anchors:**
  - GOOD: "You had the red one. You always took the red one. Lot eight."
  - GOOD: "I waited where you left me. It was warm after a while."
  - DRONE (avoid): "Sister, I have wandered the cold halls of death yearning for your touch."
  - DRONE (avoid): "Come to me, come to the water, come and be with me forever."

## Emotional Arc Tracking

### Iris Pell
- **Ch1-3 (armored competence):** control, method, mild unease filed as nerves. She is winning, or believes she is.
- **Ch4-7 (first cracks):** the count and the sealed ball breach her certainty; irritation curdling into a fear she will not name.
- **Ch8-10 (caught):** dread sharpens as the ledgers loom and the house closes the exits; from disquiet to genuine fear.
- **Ch11-12 (the wound opens):** the figure forces the buried grief up; the chapter of lowest defenses, raw rather than afraid.
- **Ch13-16 (comprehension as horror):** each decoded fact lands harder than the last; from suspicion to knowing she is next.
- **Ch17-19 (sealed and watching):** despair edged with a grim, gathering resolve; the storm takes hope of rescue, which oddly steadies her.
- **Ch20 (the cruelest test):** longing and horror at once; she nearly breaks toward the thing that wears Danny, then walks away. Peak emotional cost.
- **Ch21-22 (full knowledge, then pity):** terror at her written name; then, reading Edith, revulsion softening toward pity, which becomes her fuel.
- **Ch23-24 (refusal made physical):** ferocity, then the body's failure; she spends herself completely.
- **Ch25 (the answer she cannot hear):** absent from her own coda; the reader carries the grief she no longer can.

### Edith Vane (in arrears, via ledgers)
- Dutiful keeper (early entries, neutral) → complicit (the summer of Danny, suppressed) → faltering (the late entries, dread under the clerical surface) → kept (her own name in her own hand). Read by Iris as monster, then as a woman trapped fifty years.

## Setting/Location Details

- **Coldmere** — the Vane house, at the end of Fen Lane, eleven miles from the nearest town, Harrow's Cross (about a forty-minute drive on single track in good weather, longer in the November dark, impossible once the culvert goes). Built in the 1890s and added onto in clumsy decades, so the interior logic does not match the footprint: corridors where exterior walls should be, a landing wider than the rooms it serves. Late November. No cell signal past the third cattle-grid. A landline that gives only a dial tone, and the tone is a half-second too slow, as if something is on the line and answering late.
- **The warm room** — a small room off the upstairs hall. Not on any floor plan; absent from every photograph of the house. Measures larger inside than the wall it occupies should allow (Iris confirms this with a tape later). It is not cold. That is the wrong of it: where every other room holds the November chill, this one is blood-warm, and the warmth is the keeping. Edith's reading chair faces the single window, which looks down on the pond. The dent in the cushion refills after Iris stands. Edith was found seated here.
- **The mill-pond** — behind the house, downslope from the warm room's window, perhaps eighty yards across rough ground. Drained and re-filled over the years, now low and skinned with early ice. Where Danny went under; body never recovered. Under the ice, late in the book, a shape. Close enough to reach on foot in the dark, far enough that the walk down in a nor'easter is its own ordeal. **Spatial note for coherence:** eighty yards over rough, frozen, downhill ground in a storm is a punishing several-minute walk each way; the ch23 climax allots a full pre-dawn hour for the descent, the ice-breaking, the burning, and the failing return. No scene treats the pond as adjacent to the door.
- **Fen Lane** — a single track off the Harrow's Cross road, crossing three cattle-grids and one bridge culvert. Cell signal dies at the third grid. Late in the book a nor'easter ices the lane and brings down the culvert, washing out the one bridge and sealing Iris in. Plausible, mundane, total. The eleven miles become uncrossable not by magic but by weather and a broken culvert, which is worse. **Spatial note:** the culvert sits between Coldmere and town; once it goes, the only exit is severed, and the warm room's window faces the pond, not the lane, so Iris cannot see the road wash out, only hear and later confirm it.
- **The study** — ground floor, where Edith kept her ledgers in a glass-fronted case. The clearance work and the ledger-decoding both happen here; it is the room where Iris's catalog voice and Edith's ledger voice meet on the page.

## Timeline

Five days in late November, plus a coda. All weekday labels derive from Day 1 = Thursday.

- **Day 1, Thursday (Ch 1-4):** Iris arrives at dusk. Settles in. Finds the warm room. The counting begins.
- **Day 2, Friday (Ch 5-10):** The working day: cataloging, the child's things, the sealed lot, the ledgers surfacing, the footprints; then the failed departure at night.
- **Day 3, Saturday (Ch 11-16):** The figure at the water and the memory it wakes; the backstory of that summer; decoding the register; Danny's name; measuring the room; the climbing count.
- **Day 4, Sunday (Ch 17-22):** The storm gathers and the culvert goes; the shape under the ice; the thing that wears Danny; the final ledger and her own name; Edith's full story.
- **Day 5, Monday pre-dawn (Ch 23-24):** The climax, in the black hours before first light. The pond, the fire, the door.
- **Coda (Ch 25):** Some weeks later. The next archivist arrives. The loop closes.

### Temporal coherence checks
- The count climbs "night by night" across Day 1 (ch4), implied through Day 2, and is read as nearing its top by Day 3 evening (ch16). Three nights of climb match the three nights elapsed (Thu, Fri, Sat) before the storm.
- The date written ahead in the final ledger (ch21) is "within days," landing on Day 5 / the frost, which is exactly the night the climax occurs. The deadline and the climax coincide by design.
- Edith was found before the book opens; her body has been removed by authorities; only the chair, the dent, the photographs, and the ledgers remain. She "appears" in the warm room once (referenced ch22 / the keeper material) as the house's keeper-image, not as a living person.
- The storm (ch17 gathers, ch18 breaks) seals the lane on Day 4 afternoon, before the climax, so no rescue is possible by Day 5. The car fails earlier (ch10, Day 2 night), so weather seals what the house already closed.

## Hidden Mechanics

The house is the antagonist; its "plans" are the count's logic, hidden beneath mundane explanation until the reveals.

- **The count:** Coldmere holds a fixed register of the dead. The count must not fall short. When a kept name "settles" (is fully absorbed, the warmth in the room a marker of recent keeping) or when the keeper dies and is herself kept, the count comes up wanting one, and the house works to take the next. The numerals in the walls are the running tally; they climb toward the lot number that is the next taking.
- **The keeper's function:** a living person bound to the house writes the ledger in their own hand, names and dates, and is compelled to provide the next name when the count comes short. Edith was keeper fifty years. She fed the count (Danny among others) to keep her own name out of the book. At the end she was overdrawn: the count took her, and her last act was to write, by her own compelled hand, the next keeper-or-victim: Iris.
- **Why Iris:** blood (a Vane relation), proximity (the only one willing to come), and the perfect wound (a brother taken here, never grieved cleanly). The house does not invent her guilt; it uses it. Danny was the bait laid thirty years in advance.
- **The mundane cover at every step:** pipes and settling (the count), old wiring and a slow line (the dial tone), a misremembered floor plan (the room's size), a feral child or trespasser (the footprints, the figure), weather and infrastructure failure (the sealing). Each supernatural fact has a rational alibi Iris can reach for until she can't.
- **The dial tone's lag:** the half-second delay is the house answering on the line, present and listening; it foreshadows the thing-that-wears-Danny's half-second speech lag in ch20.
- **The refilling dent:** the warm room is occupied even when empty; the keeper's chair is never truly vacated. After ch22 the reader understands Edith is in it.
- **The trap:** burning the register (ch23) does not free the kept; it ends Iris's documented exception. The coda's fresh ledger, in a familiar hand, reveals the count simply resumed, and Iris's destruction of the book did not stop the keeping; it made her part of it. The new archivist is the next Iris.

## Structural Devices

- **The catalog frame:** chapters often open or pivot on a condition-report beat ("Condition:"), and the inventory vocabulary degrades from professional control to the prose naming Iris herself as a lot. The recurring image of her own future lot number is the spine.
- **Two hands, one rhythm:** Iris's catalog voice and Edith's ledger voice are deliberately rhymed. The dread peaks when Iris recognizes her own cadence in a dead woman's account-book (ch13-14, ch22).
- **The count as ticking clock:** the numerals climbing in the walls give the book an audible countdown to the lot that is Iris.
- **Recurring images on a strict budget:** the red rubber ball; the slow dial tone; lot numbers and the count; the dent in the cushion; the ice on the pond; Edith's handwriting; the warm-room window onto the water. Each recurs to accrue meaning, never as filler.
- **Cold/warm inversion:** the single warm room in a frozen house is the structural emblem of keeping. Warmth is wrong throughout.
- **The framed coda:** ch25 reframes the whole novella as one entry in a register, closing the loop without explanation or rescue.

## Voice Card Exceptions

### EXCEPTION: Edith Vane ledger voice uncontracted forms
- **Scope:** Edith's ledger entries and the fractured late-entry prose only (quoted matter in ch8, 13, 14, 21, 22).
- **Pattern:** Uncontracted forms ("I have," "it would not," "I am given to write," "the count is short").
- **Reason:** Voice card §2 prescribes an archaic-clerical, uncontracted keeper's register; contractions break the period account-book voice and the deliberate rhyme with Iris's catalog cadence.
- **Authority:** Authorial choice per writing-guide §4.3.

### EXCEPTION: Iris fragment-and-clip under stress
- **Scope:** Iris-POV narration during high and very-high temperature passages (ch4, 7, 10, 11, 16, 18-20, 23-24).
- **Pattern:** Verbless fragments and clipped 1-4 word sentences ("Child-sized. Mattress sound. Lot 19.").
- **Reason:** Voice card §2 prescribes that her sentences fracture under pressure; the fragments are the intended stress signature, not error. Mechanical reviewers may otherwise flag fragment density.
- **Authority:** Authorial choice per writing-guide §4.3.

### EXCEPTION: the count's bare-numeral cadence
- **Scope:** Rendered counting in the walls and the Danny-thing's numeral lapses (ch4, 16, 20, 23).
- **Pattern:** Sub-sentence units ("Lot forty. A pause. Lot forty-one.") and bare ascending numerals.
- **Reason:** Voice card §2 prescribes auction-floor minimalism; these are deliberately not full sentences. Reviewers may flag as sentence-fragments or repetition.
- **Authority:** Authorial choice per writing-guide §4.3.

### EXCEPTION: recurring motif sentences
- **Scope:** The budgeted motifs in §12 (the slow dial tone, the dent that refills, the count, Lot [number]).
- **Pattern:** Deliberate recurrence of these images/phrasings across chapters within their stated budgets.
- **Reason:** Structural Devices §10 calls for these as load-bearing recurring images; recurrence within budget is intended, not fingerprint accumulation.
- **Authority:** Authorial choice per writing-guide §4.3.

**Anti-"chosen one" trace.** Iris is not a chosen heroine; she is the available relative with the usable wound. Every resolution she reaches is a deduction from evidence the reader watched her gather: the unsquared count (ch1) plus the impossible measurement (ch15) yields "the house's space is false"; the century column (ch13) plus Danny's dated entry (ch14) plus Edith's hand on her own name (ch21) yields "the keeper writes the count and I am the next entry"; her own catalog training yields the only countermove she can imagine, destroying the inventory. The coda's reveal, that destruction was itself foreseen and absorbed, is the trap, not a withheld solution: the house always wins because the keeping does not depend on the book surviving, a fact the reader can reconstruct from ch22's keeper-succession material.

  - D5 has 2 chapters (42% of mean) — below the 50% floor.
  - D1 has 4 (83%), D2/D3/D4 have 6 each (125%) — all within tolerance.
