# What the House Keeps

*A novella. Author: **Greg Ratajik**. Genre: literary gothic horror / haunted house. Length target: ~19,000 words, 25 chapters. First person, past tense, single POV (Iris Pell). The house is named **Coldmere**.*

## Premise

Iris Pell catalogs and clears the estates of the dead for a living. Condition reports, lot numbers, a clean inventory, then the auctioneers come. When her estranged great-aunt Edith Vane dies alone and the bank moves to seize the house, Iris is the only relative willing to drive the eleven miles down Fen Lane to empty Coldmere before the sale. She has not been to the house in thirty years, not since the summer her little brother Danny drowned in the mill-pond behind it and was never found.

Coldmere is wrong in small ways first. A room off the upstairs hall that appears on no floor plan and in no photograph, and that, alone in a freezing house, is blood-warm. A child's things in a house where no child was ever born. Wet, small footprints on the stairs that dry from the outside in. Someone counting in the walls, reciting numbers in the cadence of an inventory. And in Edith's meticulous ledgers, columns of names and dates going back over a century, a housemaid in 1911, a boy in 1948, others, and near the end, in Edith's own hand, Danny's name, dated the summer he died.

The house keeps its dead. It has kept Danny for thirty years. Edith was its keeper, and now Edith is kept. The count needs one more, and the last entry in the final ledger is already written, ahead of time, in Edith's hand: Iris's name, with a date a few days out.

## Themes

- Grief as the door the house walks through. The house does not invent Iris's guilt; it uses it.
- Inventory against the uncountable. Iris narrates in the language of cataloging, condition, provenance, lot, and the house slowly turns that language against her until she is an item in it.
- What "keeping" means: not rest, not peace. Possession. The dead here are held, not at rest.
- The unrecovered body. The particular horror of a loss with no proof and no grave, and what it means to learn the loss was a taking.

## Characters

### Iris Pell
Protagonist and narrator. Female. 38. Estate-clearance archivist; she empties dead people's houses for auction houses and banks. Practical, dry, grief-armored; she keeps the world at arm's length behind a clipboard. Thirty years ago, at nine, she looked away for a moment at the pond and her brother was gone; she has organized her whole life around never looking away again, which is why she catalogs the dead. She does not believe in ghosts. She believes in provenance. The house teaches her the difference. She refers to herself rarely; when she does, the pronoun stays *she/her* throughout. The house's corruption is in the prose, never in her gender.

### Danny Pell
Iris's younger brother. Male. Drowned in the mill-pond at seven, thirty years ago; body never recovered. The child the house has held since. What appears wearing him is not him, though it knows everything he knew. He/him.

### Edith Vane
Iris's great-aunt, recently deceased. Female. Never married, no children. Found seated in the warm room, no cause of record. The house's keeper for fifty years, the one who wrote the ledgers, who fed the count, and who, at the end, was taken into it herself. She reads in the ledgers as exacting, complicit, and finally trapped. She/her. Appears only as a body, in photographs, in her handwriting, and once, terribly, in the warm room.

### The kept
The dead the house holds. Named in the ledgers: **Annie Sewell**, housemaid (female, 1911); **Thomas Reed**, a boy (male, 1948); and others, sparser, older. They are not characters so much as a register. Danny is the most recent until Edith. They do not speak so much as count.

## Setting/Location Details

- **Coldmere** — the Vane house, at the end of Fen Lane, eleven miles from the nearest town, Harrow's Cross (about a forty-minute drive on single track in good weather, longer in the November dark, impossible once the culvert goes). Built in the 1890s and added onto in clumsy decades, so the interior logic does not match the footprint: corridors where exterior walls should be, a landing wider than the rooms it serves. Late November. No cell signal past the third cattle-grid. A landline that gives only a dial tone, and the tone is a half-second too slow, as if something is on the line and answering late.
- **The warm room** — a small room off the upstairs hall. Not on any floor plan; absent from every photograph of the house. Measures larger inside than the wall it occupies should allow (Iris confirms this with a tape later). It is not cold. That is the wrong of it: where every other room holds the November chill, this one is blood-warm, and the warmth is the keeping. Edith's reading chair faces the single window, which looks down on the pond. The dent in the cushion refills after Iris stands. Edith was found seated here.
- **The mill-pond** — behind the house, downslope from the warm room's window, perhaps eighty yards across rough ground. Drained and re-filled over the years, now low and skinned with early ice. Where Danny went under; body never recovered. Under the ice, late in the book, a shape. Close enough to reach on foot in the dark, far enough that the walk down in a nor'easter is its own ordeal.
- **Fen Lane** — a single track off the Harrow's Cross road, crossing three cattle-grids and one bridge culvert. Cell signal dies at the third grid. Late in the book a nor'easter ices the lane and brings down the culvert, washing out the one bridge and sealing Iris in. Plausible, mundane, total. The eleven miles become uncrossable not by magic but by weather and a broken culvert, which is worse.

## Timeline

Five days in late November, plus a coda. All weekday labels derive from Day 1 = Thursday.

- **Day 1, Thursday (Ch 1-4):** Iris arrives at dusk. Settles in. Finds the warm room. The counting begins.
- **Day 2, Friday (Ch 5-10):** The working day: cataloging, the child's things, the sealed lot, the ledgers surfacing, the footprints; then the failed departure at night.
- **Day 3, Saturday (Ch 11-16):** The figure at the water and the memory it wakes; the backstory of that summer; decoding the register; Danny's name; measuring the room; the climbing count.
- **Day 4, Sunday (Ch 17-22):** The storm gathers and the culvert goes; the shape under the ice; the thing that wears Danny; the final ledger and her own name; Edith's full story.
- **Day 5, Monday pre-dawn (Ch 23-24):** The climax, in the black hours before first light. The pond, the fire, the door.
- **Coda (Ch 25):** Some weeks later. The next archivist arrives. The loop closes.

## Voice / Style notes (seed for the voice card)

- First person, past tense, Iris throughout. Dry, exact, controlled: a cataloguer's voice that frays as the house works on her. Her competence is the armor; the horror is watching the inventory-language turn on her ("Condition: held," "Provenance: this house," and finally her own lot number). Sentences shorten under stress; she fragments, never runs on. She counts to steady herself and the counting betrays her.
- The count is numeric, not lyrical. When the house counts in the walls it is bare numerals in auction cadence ("Lot forty. A pause. Lot forty-one"), never whispered secrets or purple menace. The horror is a mundane form, the stock-take, misapplied to the dead.
- Edith's ledger voice is clerical and uncontracted ("Annie Sewell. 1911. Settled."), and it rhymes deliberately with Iris's catalog voice; the dread is recognizing your own hand in another's book.
- Old-school restraint. Dread over gore. Scares are atmospheric and cumulative: cold and warmth inverted, wet footprints, counting in the walls, the refilling chair-dent, the shape under the ice, a photograph with one figure too many, the ledger that writes itself. Show the wrongness; do not over-explain it. At most one clear terrible image at the pond and in the warm room; the rest suggested.
- Recurring images to seed, used sparingly and on a budget, never as filler: the red rubber ball; the slow dial tone; lot numbers and the count; the dent in the cushion; the ice on the pond; Edith's handwriting.
- Watch for and avoid these machine cadences: the "not X so much as Y" frame; relentless rule-of-three lists; precious recurring abstractions; hedge-piles ("almost as if it seemed to"); regret-stacks (free-floating meditation with no on-page event). Every chapter turns on something that happens on the page.

## Critical Requirements

- **Author byline: Greg Ratajik only.** No other name anywhere.
- **Zero em-dashes** anywhere in the prose (writing-guide rule). Use commas, periods, or restructure.
- **POV/tense locked:** first person, past tense, Iris Pell, every chapter. Iris is female; her pronouns stay she/her throughout, the house corrupts the prose, never her gender. Danny is male (he/him); Edith female (she/her).
- **Withheld terms** ("the kept," "keeping," the fact Danny was taken) must not appear before their reveal chapters above.
- **Opening-type variety:** no opening type used more than 3 times across the book. **Ending-type variety:** at most 2-3 aphoristic endings total. **Emotional temperature:** no more than 3 consecutive chapters at the same temperature (the table already varies it).
- **No real brands, real places, or real people.** Harrow's Cross, Fen Lane, and Coldmere are invented.
- **The ending is a trap, not a rescue.** The coda must close the loop (the house keeps); do not resolve it into safety or explanation.
