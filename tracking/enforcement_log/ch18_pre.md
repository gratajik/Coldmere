# Chapter 18: The Culvert Goes

Rain on snow makes a sound like a man eating, and I waited in the dark kitchen and listened to the house being eaten from the outside.

It had turned at some hour I didn't mark. Snow first, the dry sideways stuff that settled nowhere, and then the rain on top of it, and the rain on the snow was the seething I'd left the window to. I kept my place at the table with the closed notebook under my hand and the marbles cold against my leg and didn't light the lamp. A finger of oil in the can is a thing you save for when you cannot do without.

I could do without, in the dark, with my hands flat on the wood and the rain going on against the black glass and the cold coming up out of the floor into my legs.

Then, under the seething, something deeper changed pitch.

I knew it the way you know a held note drop a tone. There had been a steady wash of water somewhere down the slope, the run-off finding its channels, a sound I'd stopped hearing because it had been there since the snow turned. It deepened. It thickened. It went from a wash to a working, a heavy continuous labour of water moving more than it could carry, down where the lane crossed the low ground a mile off in the dark I couldn't see into.

The culvert.

I got up. My legs were stiff and slow to take me and I rose a moment with a hand on the table until they would. Three days of bad sleep and not enough bread will do that; the body banks its complaints and presents them all at once when you ask it to stand. I went down the cold hall to the front door and put my hand on the latch and stopped.

There was nothing to see. Full dark, the rain a noise and not a sight, the lane a mile off and bending out of view past the drive. I would learn nothing in an open doorway with the cold pouring in, except the cold. But I opened it anyway, because a thing you can hear and not see is worse than a thing you can stand in the weather and confirm, and confirming is the one mercy my hands had left.

The night came in at me, wet and black and loud.

I held the step with the rain blowing in under the lintel and listened down the slope. The working sound was clearer in the open. A deep gulping labour, water gone past a channel's holding and finding new ground, with under it now a grinding I hadn't caught through the walls, stone moving on stone, the slow give of a thing built in some other decade to take a measure of water and asked, tonight, to take all of it. I counted under my breath to steady myself and stopped at three. I would not answer the slope with a count any more than I'd answer the wall.

Then it went.

I didn't see the bridge go. I have to put that down plainly, because a clearance is a record of what you witnessed and not what you supposed, and I witnessed a sound and not a sight. But a culvert giving way under a flooded lane has a sound and the sound was unmistakable: the grinding stopped, all at once, as a held thing stops resisting; and after it came a great soft collapse, a heavy wet folding-down, and then the working note of the water dropped lower still and spread wider, no longer labouring against a thing but pouring freely through where the thing had been.

The bridge over the culvert. The one bridge on Fen Lane. A mile down the slope in the dark, fallen into the flood it was built to bridge.

I kept the doorway and let the rain blow against my face and did the sum, because the sum was the only thing my hands could do and they did it without my asking. The lane runs eleven miles to the Harrow's Cross road. The lane crosses three cattle-grids and one bridge. The bridge is gone.

There is no other road. The eleven miles don't run to eleven any more; they run to a mile of flooded low ground and a broken span and then nothing a car or a person on foot could cross until the water went down, which the water wouldn't do for days, and I didn't have days.

A made-up piece. A thing whose parts no longer agree. The lane and the road were one road this morning and now they are two, with a mile of moving water between them and no joining left.

I went back in. I had to put my shoulder to the door to shut it against the wind, and shutting it was a labour, and I leaned on it after with my breath gone short and my hands flat on the cold panel and the rain still loud through the wood.

No hand but mine shut the door. I make that note again because it is the truest note I have. The house has not laid a hand on me. It didn't lock the door. It didn't break the bridge.

The weather broke the bridge, and the weather is no one's, and that is worse than a hand, because there is no hand to refuse.

I went down the hall and into the kitchen and took my place again at the table in the dark.

I took out the notebook. I had not opened it since the gates of tally-marks, and I opened it now past them to a clean page, and I held the pencil over it a long moment in the dark where I couldn't see to write, and I didn't write. There was no need to see the words. There was nothing to record that the slope hadn't already recorded for me, in stone and water, a mile off where I couldn't go.

A clearance is a list of what is in a house and a list of how each thing may be got out, and the second list had been one item long all along, the lane, and the lane was struck through now, and a list with everything on it struck through is not a list. It is just paper.

I put the pencil down. I didn't strike anything through. There was nothing left to strike.

I will say what the sum came to, because I had reached it and there is no use pretending I hadn't. The car had failed on Friday with no reason I could find. The office that answered cleanly on Friday morning had given me nothing this morning but the open listening line. The mobile had shown No service since the third grid on Thursday.

And now, on Sunday, in the rain on the snow, the one road out had fallen into the water. Four ways shut, and the fourth shut by no hand, by weather and a tired old culvert and a slope that took on more water than the pipe beneath it was built in 1890-something to take. Each one with an honest name. Together, total. A column of figures, each one true and small on its own line, comes at the bottom to a number you cannot argue with.

The house had me sealed on Friday. It hadn't needed the storm. The storm was for the world outside, to keep it from reaching in, after the fact, too late, a bolt drawn across a door already shut.

And the house knew the bolt had gone across.

I knew it because the rain changed nothing inside and the quiet changed everything. The wall had held its listening silence all through the grey afternoon, neither climbing nor resting, waiting. It was waiting still. But it was a different waiting now.

The bridge had gone and somewhere in the lath and the stone of this place the held quiet eased deeper, the way a man eases who has been watching a road and sees the last car off it and knows now that no one is coming. There was no haste in it. There had never been haste in it. A count whose top has a date does not hurry when the door is bolted and the named thing is inside it in the dark, sealed in by water it did not have to lift a finger to spill.

I bore that. I bore a long time with the closed-again notebook under my hand and the cold marbles against my leg and the rain pouring freely now through the gap where the bridge had been, a mile off, a sound that had changed from labour to a kind of ease, the slope draining at last through the broken place, taking its time.

The cold came up through the floor and into me and I let it. The bread was nearly gone and I didn't get up for the heel of it. The lamp oil was a finger and I didn't light it. There seemed no point in spending a thing against the dark when the dark had a date in it now and the date was close and there was a mile of water between me and any hand that might have reached across to pull it back.

I had come down Fen Lane on Thursday to empty a house and be gone before the sale. I had crossed the bridge that morning at last light and not thought of it twice, the way you do not think of a stair until it gives. The bridge had given. The eleven miles had given. The whole long thread back to the world, the one thread I had told myself all weekend I was still going to follow out, had been cut a mile off in the dark by no hand at all, while I waited at a table and listened to it cut.

I didn't go up to the warm room. I didn't want the window over the pond, not in the dark, not with the water everywhere outside and a shape that came to the edge of it. I stayed where I was, in the kitchen nearest the door I could no longer go out by to any purpose, and I let the lamp stay dark, and I listened to the slope drain easy through the broken place, and I didn't pick up the pencil again, and I waited for the night to go still, the way it went still before the wall began.

The wall had gone quiet in the day and I knew now it would not need to climb again before the date. It had only to wait. And so, with the road gone into the water and nothing left on the list to strike, did I.
