# Chapter 22: The Keeper

Edith's photograph came out of the drawer last, because a woman is the hardest item to read.

The lamp had a little oil yet, more than I'd thought, the can not quite a finger but enough for an hour if I kept the wick low. I'd sat with my palm flat on the closed cover till the rain went thin, and then I'd understood there was one more thing in this house I'd not let myself read: not the line with my name, which I'd read and shut, but the lines before it, the long stretch of late entries where the hand had begun to falter. I'd seen them in passing, going forward through the blank rule to find my own name. I'd not stopped on them.

A clearance isn't done while one item is read by skipping.

I opened the newest ledger again and turned back from my own line, toward Danny's, into the years between.

The hand had been the same small upright thing for a hundred years and more, the same across Annie Sewell and Thomas Reed, even-spaced, sure. It stopped being sure here. The last decade of entries had a tremble come into them, the downstrokes wavering, the letters crowding and then opening too wide, a hand that knew the shape it wanted and could no longer hold the pen quite to it. An old woman's hand. And in among the entries, where every page before had been three clean columns and nothing else, there were lines of prose now, set close in the margins and the foot, in that same crowding hand.

She had begun to write things she was not given a column for.

I read them by the low flame, slow, because a thing taken fast is a thing taken wrong.

The first was beside a name from thirty-odd years back: *I do not like the new ones. I did not used to think on them. I think on them now.* That was all.

The next, a year on, in the foot of the page below two ruled entries: *The room is warm again. I am glad of the warmth and ashamed to be glad. Fifty years and I have not learned to be cold.*

Fifty years.

I set that figure where I set figures, cold and clean, before the rest of it came. Fifty years she had kept this book. The case held fourteen books and the lowest ran back past a hundred, so she had not begun the keeping. She had taken it up, somewhere in the middle of it, a woman of thirty or so come to a house with a count already running in its walls. And she had kept it fifty years to the end of her, and the keeping had not been a thing she chose so much as a thing she'd been handed: as the pen was handed, as I had been handed her key and her floor plan and her eleven miles of lane.

There was an entry I read three times.

*The count came up wanting one. I have until the frost. I am to give it a name. I have looked at the parish and the lanes and there is no one come this year, no tramp, no lodger, no one settled near enough.

There are the two children at Coldmere for the fortnight. The boy is seven. He goes to the grounds in the mornings. I have only to leave the gate to the pond unlatched and turn away, as one turns from a thing one does not wish to see done.*

I knew the boy. I knew the grounds and the gate and the morning. I had been nine years old and reading on the bank.

*I will not write the rest until it is done,* the entry ended, *for to write it before is to do it twice.*

The next line was Danny's. His name, the year, *Kept on,* and in the margin the four words she gave them all, *the room was warm.* No prose around it. She had kept her word to herself. She had not written the rest until it was done, and when it was done she had written only the column, the clean column, the name and the year and the one word, as she'd written a hundred before him. As I write a lot when the thing is sealed and there is nothing left to say of it but its condition.

I want to set down what I felt, and the catalog has no column for it, and I will not reach for the conservator's word.

It was not forgiveness. She had unlatched a gate on a seven-year-old to keep her own name out of a book. She had stood in her rooms with my mother and let me look away on the bank and known what the looking-away was a door to, and gone on knowing it thirty years while the boy's room stayed made up at the end of the passage. There is no word in my trade or any other that makes that a thing a person does and stays whole. She did it. The doing is hers.

But I read the prose she'd crowded into the margins of fifty years, and I saw what I had not let myself see in the clean columns, which was the cost of the cleanness.

*I think on them now.* A woman who had not used to think on the names had begun to, late, when the thinking could change nothing. *I am glad of the warmth and ashamed to be glad.* She had fed a count to keep her own name out of it, and she had hated the warmth that told her she had fed it well, and she had gone on. That is not a monster. A monster does not write *ashamed* in the foot of a page where no one was meant to read.

A monster does not keep a boy's room made up. She kept it because she could not bear to clear it, and she could not bear to clear it because she knew exactly what she had cleared him into, and the made-up bed was the one thing she could give him, which was nothing, which was worse than nothing, and she gave it anyway for fifty years.

I knew that giving. I have given a list to people who could be given nothing else. I had thought it mercy. She had thought the bed mercy. We were both wrong, and we both went on.

The late entries crowded thicker toward the end, the hand worse, the prose breaking its own clerical reluctance.

*The count is short again and I am old. I have given it forty years of names and there is a thing I have not let myself write. The keeper goes into the book. I have known it from the first.

When there is no name to give, the name it takes is the one that holds the pen. I have kept my name out by keeping others in. I cannot keep others in much longer. My hand will not.*

And below it, a date, and below the date, in a hand that had steadied for one line as a hand steadies for a thing it has decided:

*I write what I am given to write.*

Then my name.

She had written it not at the end of her own strength but ahead of it, as she'd written Danny's after the gate, as the keeper writes the next before the kept one knows there's a book. She had been short by one, and old, and her hand failing, and the count would have taken her, and the only name left in eleven miles not yet in the book was the relative who would come because no one else would. She had not chosen me out of malice. She had been handed the pen one last time and told whose name to write, and she had written it, because that was the keeping. She had kept fifty years, and you do not stop being what you have been fifty years because the last name is your great-niece's.

She had written my name to buy herself nothing. There was no nothing left to buy. The count took her anyway; the warm room is warm with her this minute; the chair behind the papered seam has held her dent under no weight since the first night I pressed the cushion flat.

She wrote my name not to save herself but because the pen does not ask whether saving is possible. It asks only for the next name. And when the keeper has none to give but the one she's been writing toward all along, she writes it, and the book takes her, and the book is not finished, because the book is never finished, and the next name it wants is mine.

I closed the ledger.

I sat with it shut beneath my fingers in the last of the oil and I was not afraid of her any more. That is the strange thing I have to write. I had turned her photograph face-down out of not wanting the hard face to watch me understand it. I turned it face-up now and looked at it by the low flame: the high collar, the set mouth, the eyes that gave nothing. I had read her as a hard woman and then a monster, and she was neither, or she was both and a third thing the catalog has no column for, which is a person who did an unforgivable thing every year for fifty years and knew it was unforgivable and could not stop, and grew old inside the doing, and was taken into the doing at the close, her own name written by her own hand because there was no other to write it.

Pity is a poor word. It is the word I have. I pitied her, as you pity a thing too damaged to recover and too whole to look away from. And the pity was not soft. It had an edge I had not had since I came down the lane.

Because I knew, looking at the set mouth, that she had wanted someone to read these margins. She had crowded fifty years of shame into the feet of pages no one was meant to see, and she had left the books in a glass case with the key in the lock, for the one who came after to find. She had not wanted to be the last to know what she was.

She had wanted the next keeper to read it before the pen reached her hand.

I put the photograph face-up on the desk and left it. The window was beginning to go grey at its edge, the long night thinning toward the morning the house had laid the boy out to draw me down to. I had until the frost, the entry said. The frost was this morning. And I had, at last, the one thing Edith had wanted me to have before my own line came due, which was the whole of it, read out of her own failing hand: what the house did, and what it had made of her, and what it meant to make of me.

She had left me the cost. I stayed in the dying light and reckoned it, as she had reckoned hers, and found I was not going to pay it.
