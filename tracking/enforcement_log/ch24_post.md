# Chapter 24: The Door

Eighty yards is nothing on two feet in summer. On hands and knees in the wet of a frost morning it is a manifest of its own, item by item, each yard a thing to be got up and over and set behind me.

I went up the slope the only way that was left, palms flat on the ice-crust where the ground had frozen, knees driving, the soaked wool of my coat already stiffening to a shell. My hands had stopped being hands. I knew the fire had cooked them at the tips because I had felt it happen, but I could not feel them now, only a far-off report that they were down on the ground and bearing weight, like a thing felt through a glove two sizes wrong.

The slope was a wall.

I reckoned the yards because a thing reckoned is a thing you can finish. I got to ten and lost it and started again and lost that too, and after a while I stopped reaching for the number and only moved, one knee, one knee, the next ridge, the next.

The house stood at the top of it, blacker than the dark, one window grey where the morning was thinning. The front door was a paler oblong against the black. I fixed on it.

There is a thing you do at the close of a long clearance, when the rooms are emptied and the lots are sealed and there is one item left and you are too tired to lift it: you look at it, and you make your hands do the thing your hands no longer want to do, because the job is not done until that item is moved. The door was what remained. I crawled toward it.

Partway up, the cold finished climbing me and reached the place it had been climbing toward. The shaking stopped. That was the worst of it. I had been shaking from the pond, the deep racking shake of a body fighting the cold, and on the slope the shake went out of me and a warmth came up in its place, a false warmth, soft and full, that wanted me to lie down in the frozen ground and rest a moment, only a moment, the same warmth as the room above with the chair in it.

I knew that warmth. I had felt its weight before. It was the warmth of a thing kept, and it was in me now, and it was lying to me.

I did not lie down. I drove the next knee.

The slope eased near the top and I came onto the level of the drive, the gravel furred white, the dead car a black bulk to my left with its frosted glass. I got past it. The step was three yards on. I could see the brass door plate grey-dark, the worn shine where a thumb had pressed it a hundred years.

I got my hand up to the latch. I could not feel the latch. I watched my hand close on the dark shape of it and willed the fingers to bend, and they bent slow, late, like the line giving back its tone a half-second after you lift the receiver, and the latch lifted under a hand I could not feel doing it.

The door swung in on its silent hinge.

Warm air came out of the cold house and over me. There should not have been warm air. Every room in Coldmere held the November chill, all but the one above, and the one above was a long way up the eleven treads from this door. But warmth came out at the threshold and laid itself on my face, soft and full, the same false warmth that was in my body, and the hall beyond the door was not the dark dead hall I had crawled out of an hour before.

It was lit. Not by the lamp, which I had left burning down to nothing in the study; not by any flame. A low even light stood in the hall, the steady light of a room you have just stepped out of and mean to step back into, with no source you can name.

The cold of the open morning was at my back. The warm of the house was at my face. I knelt on the threshold with one hand on the lifted latch and the warmth coming out to meet me, and I understood what the warmth was, and what it meant that it had come down all the way to this door.

The chair upstairs warms when a name has just gone in. The room warms when a name has just gone in. And the hall was warm now, at the door, where no chair was, where no name had ever gone in, because the freshness had come down the stairs to meet the one crawling in from the cold.

I did not go in.

I want this plain, and the catalog has the column for it, which is the only mercy left to me: I knelt at the threshold of the warm house with the cold morning behind me and I did not cross. To cross was to come in out of the cold. The cold was killing me. The body that had stopped shaking and gone soft and full wanted nothing in the world but the warm, the lit hall, the door shut against the wind, the long dark rooms turned strange and welcoming.

I had crawled eighty yards up a frozen slope to reach this door because the door was out. And I knelt on the sill of it and saw that the warm hall on the other side was not out. It was the deepest in there was.

The register was ash and ruin in the pond behind me. I had burned it believing the boy would come loose, believing a thing burned is written nowhere. I knelt at the door and felt the warmth come down to meet me and knew, like the tape knowing a joint, that the burning had not stopped the warmth from coming.

The book was ash and the keeping was not. The keeping had come to the door. It had come down the stairs and across the cold hall and out to the threshold to take in from the cold the thing it had been working toward, like a man going out to the step himself when the car he has been waiting for finally turns up the drive, glad of it, warm with the welcome, the door held open.

The warmth wanted me to come in.

I knelt on the sill between the cold that was killing me and the warm that was worse than killing me, and the choosing was not the choosing I had thought I crawled up to make. I had thought the door was the choice between dying on the slope and living in the house. It wasn't. The slope behind me was the cold, and the cold was the end of me, plain, a body giving out in a frost.

The hall ahead was the warm, and the warm was the chair, and the room, and the dent that refills, and a name gone freshly in with the morning come up grey. To go in was not to live. To go in was to be warm. And being warm, in this house, is what the kept are.

I took my hand off the latch.

The door, with no hand on it, began to swing wider, slow, the warm coming out fuller, the lit hall opening itself for the one it had been kept ready for. I knelt and watched it open and the warmth pour down over me and I did not lean into it. The body wanted the warm the way the slope pulls a thing down to its low point.

I held against the pull the one way I had left, which was not strength, there was no strength in me, but only the refusal that had got me down the slope and through the fire, the same refusal I had set against the thing in the kitchen doorway wearing my brother's face. I would not be warmed. I turned my own face from the lit hall as I had turned it from his.

I got my hand back down onto the cold gravel of the step, on the outside of the sill, in the wind, in the killing cold, and I held it there, palm flat on the frozen stone, while the warm house opened its door wider behind me and waited for me to come in out of the cold.

The grey at the one window had spread. First light was coming up over the slope and the broken place a mile off where the flood still poured easy through the gap. The frost was on everything, the drive, the dead car, the worn brass plate, my own sleeve glazed with ice. The cold went into me the final stretch and the warmth in the house went on pouring out at my back, soft and full and patient, and I knelt on the threshold with my burned hand flat on the freezing step and did not go in, and could not go on, and the morning came up grey over the pond that keeps what it takes.
