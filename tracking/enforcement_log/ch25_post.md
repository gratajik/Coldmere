# Chapter 25: Condition Report

The new one comes down Fen Lane in the second week of December, with a clipboard on the passenger seat and a thermos gone cold against it.

She is Marian Webb. She has cleared the dead for nineteen years, longer than the woman before her, and she does not believe in much beyond a manifest and a hammer price. The bank's instruction is plain: the Vane estate, half-cleared by a relative who walked off the job, the sale held up, the bridge on the lane only just rebuilt. Empty what is left. Get it to the auctioneers by the New Year.

She crosses the new culvert at the third grid and the signal on her phone drops to nothing, and she notes that, because she notes everything.

The house stands grey above the track, slate and two chimneys, a brass plate worn shiny where a thumb has pressed it a hundred years. She lets herself in with the bank's key and the cold comes out to meet her, a flat weeks-old chill that has sunk into the boards.

She works as she has always worked. Room to room, clockwise from the door, the object first and then its condition and then a lot tied on a tag. The drawing room is half-done already, eleven sealed crates against the dining-room wall in another woman's hand, the tape cut square, the manifests neat. Marian reads the writing and does not think much of it beyond competent. She carries on from where it stopped.

There is a floor plan in the hall, the bank's, with a margin note on the reverse in a hand not Marian's, underlined: a yard of depth unaccounted behind the upstairs rooms. She paces the rooms by eye and finds the note holds, and sets it aside as old surveying, a wall built thick in a thick-walled age.

She does the ground floor in two days and goes up on the third.

The landing is wider than the rooms it serves. She counts the doors against the plan, five, and finds them. Then she lays her hand flat on a stretch of papered inner wall between the bathroom and the box room, where the plan draws solid, and the wall is warm.

Marian has cleared houses where the wiring ran hot in a wall, and houses where a flue went up behind plaster, and she knows the warmth a pipe leaves. This is not that warmth. There is no pipe. She finds the seam in the paper and the room behind it, the green chair faced to the window, the footstool, the side table, the lamp, and she stands in the doorway a moment longer than the standing takes.

The chair holds a body-shaped dent in its cushion, the back and the two pale forearm-worn places, pressed deep.

She flattens it with her palm, out of habit, as you square a cushion for the catalogue. By the time she has stepped back into the doorway the dent has re-formed, sinking under a weight that is not there.

Marian writes it on the reverse of the plan, in her own hand. *Room off landing, inner wall. Not on plan. Warm, no source. Chair, cushion holds the shape.* She does not underline it. She is too long at the trade to underline a thing she cannot square; she only logs it, and goes down, and leaves the door open, because her hand will not quite close it.

The study is the last room with anything left in it.

There is a desk and a chair, both tagged in the other woman's hand, and a glass-fronted case stood open and empty, the shelves swept of whatever they had held. On the desk, face-up, a photograph of a hard-faced woman in a high collar, the eyes giving nothing. Marian turns it over to read the back, finds a name and a year in pencil, *Edith Vane*, and sets it down again. The keeper's likeness, she supposes, the dead aunt. She does not know the word she has supposed.

The empty case troubles her as an empty case always troubles her, which is to say not at all and then a little. A house keeps its papers. Old families keep ledgers, accounts, a record of who came and went. Whatever stood here is missing, burned or carried off, and a half-cleared estate with its records stripped is a thing she will note for the bank and not lose sleep over.

She is gathering the desk things into a crate when she finds the new ledger.

It is in the bottom drawer, under a folded plan and a tin of dried-out pens, a plain volume with a stiff cloth cover not yet broken in. She takes it out into the grey window-light and opens it across her knee, as she opens any record she finds, to know what it is before she lots it.

Three columns, hand-ruled. A wide one, two narrow. The wide column for who, the first narrow for a year, the second for a single word.

The pages are blank.

All but the first leaf. On the first leaf, in the wide column, one entry, and Marian reads it and does not at first take it in, because it is a clearance ledger like her own, a fresh volume started in a fresh year, and there is nothing in it but a person, and the person is a woman, and beside her the year just gone, and in the third column the one word, and the word is a word she has used herself ten thousand times for a thing that is held and not let go.

*The archivist's own.*

The hand is small and upright and even. It is the same hand that wrote the neat manifests on the eleven crates downstairs, the tape cut square, the lots in their clean column, the floor-plan note underlined. The hand of the woman who came before her, who half-cleared this house and walked off the job. It is also, exactly, line for line and letter for letter, the hand on the reverse of the photograph on the desk. The dead aunt and the lost archivist share one hand, and Marian holds the volume in the cold light and cannot make that be two women.

She turns the leaf. Blank. She turns the next. Blank, and the next, the long blank rule running on, a record begun and waiting, a column with room in it.

She goes back to the one entry.

A clearance archivist starts a fresh ledger at a fresh house. That is the plain reading. The first line is the first lot. But there is no number against it, no object, no condition of an object. There is only a person, a year, and a word, and the word is one you do not give to a chair or a clock. *Kept on.* You give it to a thing taken into a house and held.

Marian Webb has cleared the dead for nineteen years and has never once been afraid in a house. She is not a woman the dark reaches. She sits in the study chair the other woman tagged, in the cold, with the ledger open across her knee and her own breath going out white, and she reads the single entry in the single hand, and the hand is the same, and the case is empty, and the chair above her is warm and holds its shape, and somewhere in the wall, faint, at the edge of hearing, there is a knock, and then a figure, and then a clean pause.

She does not, at first, count along.

She catches herself doing it and stops. One, and the pause, and two. The cadence of it is familiar, the flat patient rhythm of a man calling lots in a saleroom doorway, off a list, in order. She has heard that rhythm her whole working life. She has never heard it come out of a wall.

She tells herself pipes. She tells herself an old house in the cold, settling, the iron contracting and ticking as the heat goes out of the day. She is a practical woman and there is a practical name for everything, and she reaches for it, as you reach for the rail on a dark stair.

It goes on. It does not climb high tonight. It rests, after a while, when the wind comes back to the eaves, laid by, not finished.

Marian closes the new ledger. She sits a moment with it shut beneath her cold hand, the cover stiff, the binding sound, no woodworm, the sort of volume that will take a hundred years of entries and not give out. She has a column for it. *One ledger, blank but for the first line, found bottom drawer.* She does not write that down. Her pencil is in her pocket and her hand is on the cover and she does not take the pencil out, and she could not have told you why, except that to enter it in her own hand would be to enter it.

The light is failing at the one window. Below the house, downslope, a low oval of water lies under a skin of new grey ice, and there is a place near the middle of it, deep, where the ice has gone milky over something, a darker place with an edge to it, about a yard across, that she has not gone down to look at and will not, tonight.

She will stay in the kitchen, nearest the door, with the lamp turned low against the dark she does not like. She will finish the clearance in the morning. It is a clean job, or near enough, only the records taken and a warm room not on the plan and a ledger in the bottom drawer with one entry in it, and the rest of the pages blank, and the case in the study standing open and empty and waiting to be filled.

She takes the new ledger down to the kitchen with her, because you do not leave a record of the house lying loose in a house you are clearing, and you keep your manifests to hand. She sets it on the table by the lamp, the cover shut, the one entry inside it, the long blank column under it.

Then she sits, with her cold hands flat on the cold wood, as the woman before her must have sat, in the same chair, by the same lamp, listening to the same wall. The morning is coming down Fen Lane toward her. It came for the others the same way, in a hand not yet her own.
