### CRITICAL ISSUES

---

**C1**
**Chapters:** 23
**Category:** Grounding & Mechanism Plausibility (Category 9) / Book-Specific Validation 8d
**Severity:** Critical
**Description:**

The climax depends on a causal mechanism the story establishes nowhere: burning the register frees the kept. Iris's whole act of destruction rests on this logic — "Unmake the inventory and you unmake the keeping. Burn the book and the boy comes loose." But story.md's Hidden Mechanics are explicit and opposite:

> *"Burning the register (ch23) does not free the kept; it ends Iris's documented exception. The coda's fresh ledger, in a familiar hand, reveals the count simply resumed, and Iris's destruction of the book did not stop the keeping; it made her part of it."*

The trap is designed to close *because* burning fails — the coda is the revelation that it failed. The problem is not that Iris is wrong (she should be wrong; she is always wrong). The problem is that *the prose asserts her reasoning as if it is mechanically sound*: "A thing burned is not written anywhere" is stated as a discovered fact of how the house works, not as a desperate hope. It is stated with the same authority as her tape-measure deductions — and those deductions were correct. The narrative voice does not signal that this deduction is the one that fails. A reader following Iris's impeccable detective work through ch13–16 will read "burn the book and the boy comes loose" as the correctly-reasoned climax solution, making the coda's reversal feel like a late rules-change rather than an earned trap.

Additionally, the burned-and-drowned method (fire *and* water) is introduced in ch23 as Iris's own improvised insight with no earlier setup. The idea that drowning ash prevents reassembly has no in-world grounding — it is an escalation with no textual basis.

**Suggested fix:**
In ch23, distinguish Iris's *hope* from the narrative's implied endorsement of her logic. Cut or reframe the line "Unmake the inventory and you unmake the keeping. Burn the book and the boy comes loose." Replace with something that keeps the motivation (she *believes* this, desperately, because it is the only thing her catalog-logic can reach for) while signaling to the careful reader that belief and mechanism are different. For example: frame it as the only theory she has, not as a deduction she has earned the way she earned the tape-measure reading. The fire-and-water elaboration ("burned and drowned is finished both ways, past gathering") should similarly be marked as Iris's hope, not a discovered rule. This preserves the coda's power — the trap closes *because she was wrong about the mechanism* — while making the wrongness legible in retrospect.

**C2**
**Chapters:** 24
**Category:** Grounding & Mechanism Plausibility (Category 9) / Plot & Story Arc (Category 2)
**Severity:** Critical
**Description:**

Ch24 presents an ungrounded capability: the warm house extends itself all the way to the front-door threshold and produces lit, warm air in the cold hall. The warm room's established mechanics are:

- A *specific small room* off the upstairs landing, behind a papered seam, not on any plan
- Warm because it is the place of keeping; its warmth is the mark of a fresh name

Nowhere in the story is it established that the keeping's warmth can travel. Every prior chapter establishes the cold hall, the cold kitchen, the cold stairs. The warmth is bounded by the warm room itself — that is structural to the horror (the wrongness of *one* warm room in a frozen house). In ch24 the house suddenly has the ability to extend warmth down two flights and into the front hall as an active welcoming lure. No mechanism in story.md accounts for this. It presents as magic: the house *decides* to warm the door to draw Iris in.

Further: the lit hall ("a low even light stood in the hall, the steady light of a room you have just stepped out of") has no established source. The lamp was burned out in the study. The power was failing. There is no established in-world mechanism by which the house produces sourceless light in an unlit space. This is the antagonist performing a feat (warmth + light migration) that the book's own mechanics never granted it.

**Suggested fix:**
Comply with the established mechanism. The house does not act through environmental transformation of unestablished spaces; it acts through the warm room and the count and the ledger. Cut the extended warmth and the sourceless light from the hall. Replace the lure at the threshold with something the established mechanics support: Iris reaches the front door and finds it already open a crack — as if someone inside left it ajar for her (the house's passive readiness, not an active welcome). The cold air pours through the gap. She understands she is expected. The choosing remains: cross the threshold into the cold-inside-the-open house, or turn away. The horror of the threshold scene does not require the house to produce new powers; it requires Iris to choose.

**M1**
**Chapters:** 12
**Category:** Plot & Story Arc (Category 2) / Book-Specific Validation 8e (Who-Knows-What)
**Severity:** Major
**Description:**

Ch12 ("That Summer") deploys the backstory at a moment that precedes its proper informational trigger. Per the Who-Knows-What matrix and the emotional arc, the figure at the water (ch11) is the event that "wakes the thing she has not let herself look at in thirty years" — and ch12 is explicitly the backstory chapter triggered by ch11. That sequencing is correct and working.

The problem is the secret she carries: *"that the moment she looked away she was watching her own reflection in the water, not Danny."* This secret is story.md's explicit depth-charge for ch12. But the prose gives it away earlier than it should be given away. In ch11, the narration already reads:

> *"I turned from the glass. The job doesn't want that and neither do I. I have never cared for my reflection in a dark window, for thirty years now, since a particular afternoon when the thing I was watching in the water was my own face and not the boy beside me, and the water closed over what I should have been watching while I looked at myself."*

This is in ch1, not ch11 — the quoted passage is from ch1's hall-window scene. The thirty-year shame about the reflection is disclosed in full in ch1, long before ch12's "lid up" moment. By the time ch12 arrives, the reader has already been told the deepest truth. Ch12's "I hadn't told the rest" and "I'll tell it now" land as a formal re-announcement of something already disclosed.

**Suggested fix:**
Revise ch1's dark-window passage to withhold the specificity. Iris can register aversion to her reflection without explaining the reason — the aversion itself is the tell; the content of the shame belongs to ch12. Cut the clause "since a particular afternoon when the thing I was watching in the water was my own face and not the boy beside me, and the water closed over what I should have been watching while I looked at myself" from ch1. Trim to: "I have never cared for my reflection in a dark window, for thirty years now." The content arrives where the bible places it: ch12's drawer opening.

**M2**
**Chapters:** 20
**Category:** Book-Specific Validation 8e (Who-Knows-What) / Character (Category 3)
**Severity:** Major
**Description:**

The thing wearing Danny names the marble and the act of keeping it ("You had the red one … I let you keep it that summer and you never gave it back") as the proof no living person could know. But the story has established, in ch6, that Iris *took the marbles off the shelf and put nineteen of them in her pocket*, and that she consciously counted them and noted the red one. She has been carrying them against her leg for three full days. The marbles — including the red marble with the white twist — are a physical object she has handled repeatedly and thought about repeatedly throughout the manuscript.

The Danny-thing's citation of the marble is meant to be the thing-that-knows-one-detail-too-many, the proof of inhuman accuracy. But Iris herself has been fixated on the marble since ch6. A skeptical reader can reasonably wonder whether the house, which has demonstrated access to Iris's interior (it reads the wound), is simply reflecting her own guilt back at her rather than recalling a genuine private memory. This is ambiguous in a productive way — except that the prose presents the marble-citation as unambiguous proof ("No one alive knew that"), which forecloses the ambiguity too cleanly and raises a different question: how does the house know which marble Iris is carrying in her pocket in a way that "no living person" could know, when Iris herself has been holding it and thinking about it for seventy-two hours?

The deeper problem: the detail the prose presents as the clinching proof of inhuman knowledge ("You always took the red one. The one with the white in it. I let you keep it that summer and you never gave it back") is precisely the detail Iris has been rehearsing in her own interior narration. The *kept* marble from ch6 and ch12 is already interior-narrated. For this to land as "no one alive knew that," it needs to be something Iris has never narrated — a detail truly sealed in the ch12 drawer.

**Suggested fix:**
Keep the marble as the lure-object but change the clinching proof of inhuman knowledge to something Iris has *not* rehearsed in the narration — a specific private exchange between her and Danny that never appears in any prior chapter interior, or the precise words Danny said the moment before he went under (something Iris registered but suppressed even in ch12's full confession). The prose can still use the marble as the *emotional* hook (the thing the lure raises to draw her), but the *logical* proof that this is not merely the house reading her own guilt should rest on a detail the narration has kept from the reader alongside keeping it from Iris's conscious mind.

**M3**
**Chapters:** 23, 24
**Category:** Character Condition Ledger / Grounding (Category 9)
**Severity:** Major
**Description:**

Story.md's Character Condition Ledger is explicit about ch23–24:

> *"Ch23: Hypothermic, burned hands, soaked, spent — Barely mobile; cannot run; failing fast."*
> *"Ch24: Crawls/staggers the eighty uphill yards back; at the threshold she is at the absolute limit of her body — Cannot stand unaided; consciousness failing."*

The prose honors this for the descent and the breaking of the ice. But the return in ch24 describes hands that "would not shut as fingers shut" and legs that "wouldn't put me on my feet" — and then Iris performs a complex multi-step action: she gets "both arms over the lip," hauls against a reed-root with burned fingers that "won't bite into it," slides, goes in to the chest, and then *climbs out unaided*, rolls onto the bank, rises to her knees, and crawls the full eighty yards up the slope alone, ending the chapter on the doorstep performing a deliberate action (taking her hand off the latch, placing her burned palm flat on the step outside the sill).

The condition ledger says she cannot stand unaided and consciousness is failing. The prose has her performing sustained purposeful actions, including a final act of will (pressing her palm to the outside stone) that reads as deliberate choice rather than collapse. The ledger's stated limit — "barely mobile," "cannot stand unaided" — is not honored: she rises to her knees and crawls eighty yards. That is consistent with the ledger. But the drawn-out final deliberation at the door (a long scene of active resistance and choosing) requires cognitive clarity and motor control the condition ledger explicitly denies her.

**Suggested fix:**
The door scene can remain; the condition ledger allows for a final moment of will. But compress the physical deliberation. Iris should be at the margin of consciousness, not staging a careful analysis of warmth-as-keeping. The realization at the threshold can be present but should feel fractured, arriving in the clipped fragments her stress register uses, not in the full analytical paragraphs the current ch24 gives her. Specifically, cut the long explanatory passage beginning "Because Danny didn't know about the warm room" (which is ch20's discovery, not ch24's) and the extended meditation on choosing versus dying. Replace with: Iris feels the warmth, her body leans toward it, something in her — the last of the cataloger — reads the warmth as wrong, and her hand comes off the latch. The refusal should cost almost everything she has left to name. Then the image closes the chapter.

**M4**
**Chapters:** 25
**Category:** Plot & Story Arc (Category 2) / Book-Specific Validation 8d
**Severity:** Major
**Description:**

Ch25's trap depends on two simultaneous revelations landing for the reader: (1) the loop closed, Iris is kept, and (2) the fresh ledger is in Iris's hand — "in a familiar hand." Story.md states the coda answers whether Iris escaped through "the new ledger, not her body." The ledger is the proof.

But the trap requires the reader to identify Iris's hand as the hand in the new ledger. The narrative sets this up by having Marian compare the ledger hand to the manifests on the sealed crates (the other woman's work) and to the pencil note on the photograph's reverse. The photograph hand is identified as Edith's. The manifests are identified as Iris's. Marian concludes "the dead aunt and the lost archivist share one hand" — which she cannot make "be two women."

The logic works, but it depends on Marian having seen Iris's handwriting on the manifests *and* Edith's handwriting on the photograph, and recognizing them as the same. This is the resolution mechanism. The problem: Edith's handwriting appears in the ledgers (which the narrative explicitly says were stripped from the case — "whatever stood here is missing, burned or carried off"), so Marian has no access to the ledger hand for comparison. She has only the photograph pencil notation ("Edith Vane," a year) — a very small sample — against the new ledger's single entry. And Iris's handwriting appears only on crate manifests and the floor-plan margin note. These are the only samples Marian has for the identification.

This is a thin basis for the central revelation of the entire book. A professional archivist comparing two samples — a pencil note on a photograph back and a ledger entry — might well not make the identification with confidence, and the reader following Marian's reasoning may find it underbuilt.

**Suggested fix:**
Add one additional grounding beat. Give Marian a fuller sample of each hand. Option A: the study desk holds Iris's clearance notebook (which she left behind — the notebook is established throughout as always in her hand, and its fate after ch24 is never accounted for). Marian finds it open to a page of entries in Iris's hand and compares it directly to the new ledger. The identification becomes unmistakable and the reader sees exactly what Marian sees. Option B: Edith's handwriting appears somewhere other than the stripped ledgers — the photograph's reverse note is thin, but if there are household papers (an old receipt book, a letter in the study drawer, the faded label inside the glass case) Marian also handles, the two-hand comparison becomes more solid. Either addition is surgical and lands the trap cleanly.

**m2**
**Chapters:** 10
**Category:** Continuity (Category 1)

**Description:**

In ch10, Iris tries the car "four more times" after the initial failed attempt, for a total of five. She counts them: "I tried it four more times. Four. **m3**
**Chapters:** 15
**Category:** Grounding — setting geometry (Category 9 / 8h)
**Severity:** Minor
**Description:**

In ch15, Iris measures the warm room at "fourteen feet and two inches, window wall to door." She compares it to the box room and bathroom, both running "nine feet." The escalation from ~3' to ~5'2" between the downstairs estimate and the upstairs measurement is not addressed. **Suggested fix:**
This is either intentional (the downstairs estimate was rough, the upstairs measurement is exact, and the discrepancy is part of how the house expands its own impossible depth) or a minor continuity slip. If intentional, add one beat in ch15 where Iris notes the difference between the downstairs estimate and the upstairs measurement — this would read as a discovery and deepen the horror (the room is deeper than even her first sense of it). If unintentional, revise ch1's margin note to "approx 1.5 yds" to better align with the five-foot measurement. Either is surgical.

## A. Prioritized Issue List

### CRITICAL Issues

None. The plot logic holds. ## B. Publisher & Reviewer Panel

### 1. Acquisitions Editor

This is a tightly controlled literary gothic with a genuinely fresh engine. The conceit — an estate-clearance archivist who narrates the dead in the language of lot numbers and condition reports, slowly becoming an item in someone else's inventory — is the kind of premise that sells a novella on a one-line pitch. The bureaucratic-dread register (an auction-floor count instead of a spectral chorus, a household account-book instead of a curse) is the differentiator; it's *The Haunting of Hill House* by way of an insurance ledger, and that flatness-where-you-expect-poetry is the book's signature and its selling point. Comps: Shirley Jackson, obviously; Susan Hill's *The Woman in Black* for the cold-house machinery; Catriona Ward and Andrew Michael Hurley (*The Loney*) for the literary-horror shelf where this belongs.

At ~19K words it's a hard commercial length — too long for most magazines, too short for a standalone hardcover — so positioning is the real question. This wants to anchor a single-author collection, run in a prestige horror anthology, or be packaged as a novella with strong design. The prose quality is high enough to carry it into the literary-horror conversation rather than the mass-market one, which is the right room for it.

The one commercial caution: the narrator's verbal tic of constantly announcing that she's recording/setting-down/wanting-it-on-the-record (M1) is the kind of thing a sharp editor will circle on page two and keep circling. It's the most "samey" texture in an otherwise distinctive voice, and trimming it is the single highest-leverage polish before submission.

### 2. Developmental Editor

Structurally this is exemplary. The escalation is patient and *earned*: a count that won't square (ch1), a room that's warm (ch3), a count in the walls (ch4), a childless house's child (ch6), a sealed crate violated (ch7) — each wrongness is logged, rationalized, and then the rationalization is taken away. The "mundane cover at every step" doctrine pays off beautifully: the dead car, the slow line, the storm, the broken culvert are each individually plausible and collectively a sealing, and the prose never cheats by making the house lock a door. "The house hadn't laid a hand on me. It hadn't needed to" (ch10) is the thesis of the whole horror and it's dramatized, not stated.

The emotional architecture is the book's deepest achievement. The reveal in ch14 — that Iris's lifelong guilt was misdirected, that there was no drowning to prevent, that she's "stood guard thirty years over a death I couldn't have stopped" — recontextualizes the whole grief engine without letting her off the hook, and the prose is careful to insist on that ("A girl who admired her own face in the pond while her brother went under does not get let off"). The pivot from monster-Edith to pitied-Edith in ch22 is handled with real maturity; the late-ledger marginalia ("I am glad of the warmth and ashamed to be glad") does the work that a lesser draft would have done with a monologue.

My only structural note is the back-half cadence of refusal (m4/M1): chapters 11, 19, 20, and 24 each build to a "she did not go down / she did not cross / she did not turn" beat, and each is prefaced with the same "I want this plain" throat-clear. The refusals themselves are the spine of the climax and they work — but the *framing* repeats, and repetition of frame flattens the curve the content is trying to raise. Strip the prefaces; let two or three of those refusals land without announcement.

### 3. Copy Editor

The mechanical discipline here is unusually clean. I found zero em-dashes — a genuine achievement given the fragmenting-speech demands of this material; the period-separated fragments do the work em-dashes would have done ("This is. It's really."), exactly per the guide's workaround. Dialogue tags are "said"/"asked" only. Contraction discipline is correct and *meaningful*: Iris contracts in narration ("I don't believe in ghosts," "I'm paid to empty it"), Edith's ledger voice does not ("I have looked," "it would not"), and the two registers are deliberately rhymed so the dread peaks when Iris hears her own cadence in a dead woman's book. That's craft, and it's executed.

The recurring habits that need attention are two, both already named: the "set it down / on the record / I want that plain" family (M1) and the "the way X verbs" simile frame (M2). Both are *in-voice* — that's the trap. Iris is a cataloguer; "I write it down" is characterization. But the bible itself budgets these tight and flags the first as a cross-book model fingerprint, and the manuscript has sailed past both ceilings. The fix is not to neuter the voice but to thin to budget and vary the survivors. Replace a third of the "on the record" beats with the bare action ("I wrote it") or with nothing; swap a third of the similes for direct statement.

Smaller watch-items: "quiet/held/listening" clustering in ch17–18 (m3), and the interior-construct "part of me" family nudging its budget in ch11–12 (m1). None of these is a mechanical *error*; they're density issues, and density is the 2025 tell. One genuinely fine touch worth preserving: the prose lets sentences run long and ungainly exactly where the guide asks (the eighty-yard crawl in ch24, Edith's compounding shame in ch22), and lets them fracture exactly where stress demands. The variance is real, not artificial.

### 4. Genre-Savvy Beta Reader

I read this in one sitting and the dread built correctly the whole way. The single best decision in the book is that the haunting *counts*. A whispering chorus of the dead would be ordinary; a flat auction-floor voice calling lot numbers in the wall, and the slow horror of realizing the number it's climbing toward is *you* — that got under my skin in a way a thousand spectral hisses wouldn't. "I wasn't the one taking the inventory. I was the last entry in it, not yet called" (ch16) is the line where I put the book down and breathed.

Where my engagement was highest: the sealed-crate violation (ch7), because it breaks the rule the narrator has staked her whole identity on; the ch14 reveal, which is a true gut-punch *because* it doesn't comfort her; and the ch20 confrontation, where the horror is "how badly she wants it to be him." That chapter is the emotional climax and it works — the thing knowing the stolen marble, knowing she watched her own reflection, and then *slipping into a lot number mid-sentence* — that lapse is the single most chilling beat in the book.

Where my attention flagged, mildly: the middle of the back half develops a rhythm of "saw the wrong thing / refused to go down / wrote it in the notebook / didn't, actually, write it" that I started to anticipate. The refusals are powerful individually but the *shape* recurs (M1/m4), and once I clocked "I want that plain" as the tell that a refusal was coming, it lost a little of its ambush. The coda (ch25) is the right ending — the loop closing on a new archivist in the same hand is exactly the cold trap this book earns — but the final ledger line stages its reveal twice (M4); I wanted Marian's own name in that wide column, hard, instead of the abstract gloss.

### 5. Adversarial Reviewer

Let me be brutal where I can, because the book mostly survives it. The weakest texture in this manuscript is the narrator's compulsion to *narrate her own narration*. Open the fingerprint table for "Narrator announcing the act of recording" — this book is the case study. "I want it on the record." "Let that stand on the record." "I'll set it down plainly because plainly is the only way I can stand to." "I want that plain, as I have wanted every refusal plain." "The record will show I do not." It is *everywhere*, and the bible's own Voice Card flags it as a model fingerprint that recurs across unrelated books "with narrators who have no reason to talk like a clerk." Iris has a reason — but a justified tic indulged forty times is still a tic, and §3.6's "named signature reads as a license" trap has clearly sprung here. This is the thing that, uncut, marks the prose as machine-comfortable underneath its competence. Cut it by two-thirds.

Second target: the "the way X does Y" simile is the most persistent AI tell the guide names, and it's over budget. "The way old houses stand." "The way you tell a dog to sit." "The way the tide goes off a stone." "The way a marriage of parts comes clear." Some are lovely. The problem is volume — the frame becomes the default reach for any comparison, and §3.5 warns that fixing the frame without thinning the underlying habit just relocates it. Thin it.

Third, smaller: a couple of telling-after-showing gloss-sentences in the climax (M3) where the prose, having delivered a perfect cold image, can't resist explaining the image. "The accuracy was the wrong of it and the number was the deepest part of the wrong" — you already showed me that two sentences ago with the lot number. Trust the image. Delete the gloss.

Now what works, because I won't pretend it doesn't: the engine is genuinely original and *consistently executed* — the bureaucratic dread never once tips into gothic-poetic mush, which is the discipline most books in this lane fail. The grief reveal is structurally daring and doesn't flinch from the narrator's culpability. The "house didn't need a hand on you" sealing is dramatized rather than asserted. The contraction-rhyme between Iris's catalog and Edith's ledger is real craft. And the count-as-clock gives the back half an audible ticking that most quiet-horror novellas lack entirely. This is a strong book with one over-indulged verbal habit standing between it and excellent. Cut the throat-clears and it's there.

## Step 1: Voice Card Extraction

**Iris Pell (narrator, all chapters)**
- Sentence length: 9–16 words at rest; collapses to 3–7 under stress
- Structure: declarative, fragment-leaning, hard; never run-on; clips under pressure
- Vocabulary: technical-plain, estate-clearance lexicon (lot, provenance, condition, foxing, seal, manifest)
- Metaphor domain: cataloging, auction, archival conservation, inventory
- Contractions: always (human character); max 2 uncontracted per chapter
- Stress response: sentences shorten and fracture; drops verbs; retreats to catalog forms
- Forbidden: run-ons, ornamental language, figurative drift without resistance, literary musing in her own voice

**Edith Vane (ledger voice — quoted matter only)**
- Sentence length: 3–8 words; clerical fragments
- Vocabulary: archaic-clerical, uncontracted, account-book register

- EXCEPTION HONORED: uncontracted forms throughout are authorial choice

**The count / the house (non-human)**
- Sentence length: 1–4 words; bare numerals
- Vocabulary: auction cadence, no metaphor
- Forbidden: whispering, menace, lyric register

**The Danny-thing (non-human)**

## Step 2: Voice Card Compliance Audit

### Iris Pell — Contraction Compliance

The automated scan flags 16 chapters with uncontracted forms over budget (max 2/chapter). I read through each chapter and assessed scope.

**The complication:** Iris's voice card specifies she always contracts. However, a significant portion of the flagged uncontracted forms fall inside:
1. Her *recorded logging voice* — when she writes in her notebook or on manifest backs, a slightly more formal written register is plausible
2. *Reported thought* — first-person past-tense narration where she is recounting what she understood, felt, or deduced

Neither of these is an EXCEPTION in the bible. The bible's EXCEPTION covers only Edith's ledger voice and the bare-numeral count cadence. Iris's uncontracted narration is not whitelisted.

I checked a representative spread across the worst-offending chapters and found a clear pattern: the vast majority of uncontracted forms appear in her reflective, analytical narration — particularly in the higher-temperature chapters where she is deducing, reckoning, or recording the unbearable. Examples from the chapters with the highest counts:

## Step 3: Voice Convergence Detection

### Strip-the-Name Blind Test

**Passage A (calm, mid-chapter):**
> The work is the same in every house. I've done it three hundred times and I'll tell it plain, because the plainness of it is the only thing that ever stood between me and the houses I empty. You take the object. You note what it is. You note its condition. You give it a lot number and you mark the number on a tag and you tie the tag to the object, and then it is no longer a chair a person sat in or a clock a person wound. It is Lot 31, mahogany long-case, movement seized, and it belongs to the sale.

**Passage B (calm, mid-chapter):**
> The drive curved past a low stone wall, and there, down the slope to the right, was the pond. I hadn't let myself think about the pond on the way out. It was smaller than I remembered, low in its banks, skinned with the first thin ice of the year. I looked at it for the length of time it takes to confirm a thing exists, and then I looked at the house, because the house was the job and the water wasn't.

**Passage C (stressed, high-temperature):**
> Child-sized. Mattress sound. Lot 19.

**Identification:** A = Iris (ch5) — confidence: high; the lot-catalog diction, the plain imperative syntax, "You take the object / you note / you give" cadence, lot-number reference, estate-clearance lexicon. B = Iris (ch1) — confidence: high; the physical inventory-eye ("skinned with the first thin ice"), the emotional withholding, the declarative plainness. C = Iris (ch6 or similar stress peak) — confidence: high; the fragment-clip signature under stress, the lot number as armor.

**Result:** All three passages are distinctly and unmistakably Iris. Since this is a single-POV novella (Iris narrates all chapters), the convergence question operates differently: the risk is not cross-character convergence but *intra-character flattening* (Iris's voice becoming uniform across moods/chapters). Testing this below.

### Intra-Character Convergence (Iris across moods)

I compared Iris's calm-armored passages (ch1, ch5) with her cracked-open passages (ch11, ch12, ch20) and her stress-clipped passages (ch7, ch9, ch16).

**Calm passages** average 12–16 words per sentence, declarative, catalog-dense, emotion kept at arm's length. Consistent with voice card.

**Stress passages** (ch9, ch16, ch23) correctly fragment: "Child-sized. Mattress sound." / "Not the warm of a thing in a warm room." / "The floor was gone." These are 3–7 words, verb-dropping, clippped. Consistent with voice card.

**Raw passages** (ch11, ch12, ch20): some drift toward slightly more literary syntax. Example from ch11: *"The longing to go down and the terror of going down were the same size, and that is the thing I can't explain, that I wanted to go to it as much as I have ever wanted anything, that some part of me would have walked into the pond after it gladly, gladly, and that I shook at the glass with my whole body and didn't."* — This sentence runs to 60+ words, a genuine run-on. The voice card says "Never run-on. Fragment-leaning, hard." The EXCEPTION for fragment-and-clip under stress does not whitelist run-ons; it whitelists fragments. A high-stress chapter producing a 60-word run-on is a **voice card violation**.

However, the run-on is emotionally motivated (the lid of the drawer is fully open; the syntax miming the overflow of thirty years). Given Rule 10 ("let one sentence per chapter run one clause too long") and the EXCEPTION whitelist for stress passages, I'm treating this as a **minor voice drift** rather than a hard violation — it is a single sentence and serves the intended breakdown. The pattern bears watching.

**The Marian Webb coda (ch25):** Marian has no voice card and her narration is third-person limited (not first-person). The writing guide Rule 4 requires human characters to always contract. Third-person narration for a human POV should also follow this rule. With 21 uncontracted forms in ch25, Marian's narration is systematically uncontracted. Since Marian is a new human POV introduced in the coda, the text defaults her into Iris's documentary register — a form of Iris-bleeding. This is a **convergence issue**: Marian reads like Iris in her controlled-recording mode, not like a distinct character.

## Part 6: Reader Pass (first-read experience)

I read the manuscript straight through. Here is the reader's experience.

This book works. It is a slow, exacting, genuinely dreadful haunting, and the catalog-voice premise pays off into the trap with real force. The reveals are correct and earned; the *air around them* is over-cleared. The fix is compression, not cutting beats.

**Three confrontation chapters share an architecture** — 11 (figure at the water), 19 (shape under the ice), 20 (the thing in the house) — and 11 and 19 are close enough in shape (Iris at the warm-room/drawing-room window, sees a child-sized form at/under the pond, reasons through deer/trespasser, refuses to go down) that 19 reads partly as a re-run of 11 with the ice thickened. 20 is distinct and devastating. The cure is to differentiate 19's entry, not to lose it.

**The "no hand but mine shut the door" beat lands hard once (ch10) and then recurs** in 18 and 24 with diminishing force. It's the book's thesis-image — the house never touches her — and it's strongest the first time.

The slow dial tone, the dent that refills, "the water keeps what it takes," and the marble are all handled beautifully and should not be touched. The first sentence is a genuine invitation. Momentum holds through the storm; the climax and door are paced right.
